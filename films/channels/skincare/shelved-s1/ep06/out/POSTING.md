**Description**

```
Why do we get goosebumps? We hardly have fur.

So the bumps look like a leftover. But a team at Harvard found that nerve also reaches the stem cells that grow hair, and the muscle holds it in place. So why do we still get goosebumps? Because the muscle that raises a hair still looks after it.

Series: Season 1: The organ you live inside, part 6 of 6.

Sources
1. Humans look nearly hairless compared with other apes: most human body hair is fine, short vellus hair rather than visible fur. — NIH: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3367735
2. Most body hair follicles have an arrector pili, a tiny smooth muscle attached to the follicle; when it contracts the hair stands up and the skin around it is pulled into a bump (piloerection, goosebumps). — encyclopedia.com: https://www.encyclopedia.com/medicine/diseases-and-conditions/pathology/gooseflesh
3. Piloerection is an involuntary reflex driven by the sympathetic nervous system, triggered by cold or by fear (strong emotion). — NIH: https://www.nih.gov/news-events/nih-research-matters/what-goosebumps-are
4. In furred mammals, erected hair traps a layer of air near the skin, which insulates and helps keep the animal warm. — livescience.com: https://livescience.com/32349-what-causes-goose-bumps.html
5. Shwartz et al. (2020, Hsu lab, Harvard) found that the sympathetic nerves that drive the arrector pili also form synapse-like contacts with hair follicle stem cells and regulate them through norepinephrine (noradrenaline). — NIH: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32679029/
6. When the arrector pili muscle cells were removed, the nerve–stem cell connections were lost, and stem cell activation and new hair growth were delayed (mice). — sciencenews.org: https://www.sciencenews.org/article/goose-bumps-could-boost-hair-growth
7. Vellus hairs are by definition less than 30 µm in diameter (terminal hairs are typically about 60–80 µm). — donovanmedical.com: https://donovanmedical.com/hair-blog/2017/8/28/vellus-hairs-on-the-scalp
8. Standard 80 gsm copy paper is about 100 µm thick; a vellus hair (< 30 µm, c12) is under 0.3 of it ('thinner than a third', computed). 'Most of the hairs' rests on c6 (most body follicles grow vellus hair). — resource.download.wjec.co.uk: https://resource.download.wjec.co.uk/vtc/2016-17/16-17_1-4/website/category/5/1papers_and_boards/category_and_standards/index.html
```
