# Research: sunscreen vs no sunscreen, after about ten years

Accessed 2026-11-02. Every figure below was checked through search excerpts (abstracts, the ACP press release,
clinician summaries, AAD pages). Page fetches are blocked in this session (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov does not
resolve), so a person must open each source before posting. Full ledger: `claims.json`.

## The strongest angles

1. **A town split by chance:** Nambour, Queensland, 1992: a coin-flip decided who wore sunscreen every morning, and
   the same people were followed for 14 years. One cohort, three outcomes (ageing, SCC, melanoma).
2. **Invisible first:** the ageing difference was read from silicone casts of the backs of hands, not by eye, and UV
   cameras show damage the naked eye misses; skin keeps a record before it shows.
3. **The melanoma result, honestly:** "halved" is the headline, but it is 11 vs 22 cases and P = .051; the invasive
   subset (3 vs 11) is significant. An episode about how strong the evidence is.
4. **Ageing as the vanity hook:** 24% less likely to show increased ageing in 4.5 years, in adults under 55.

## Chosen angle, and why it stops a scroll

Angle 1 told through angle 2: two model hands that look the same, and the question "Which hand aged?" The answer
comes from a randomised town, casts that saw what eyes could not, and ten more years of counting; the takeaway calls
back: "the eye can't say, but the skin was keeping its own record." It stops a scroll because frame 0 is a puzzle
the viewer can try (and can't win by looking), and the stakes climb from wrinkles to melanoma without a single word
of advice.

## Disputed or uncertain

- **Melanoma "about half" is not quite significant** (CI crosses 1, P = .051), from 33 cases; melanoma was a
  secondary endpoint. The film says "in small numbers" and badges it "not quite significant". Only invasive
  melanoma (14 cases) reached significance.
- **SCC depends on the count:** significant by tumours, not by persons, during the trial; significant after
  follow-up (0.62, 0.38–0.99). Two different CIs (0.43–0.98, 0.45–0.94) appear in secondary sources for the
  follow-up estimate; the film states no SCC figure.
- **Ageing:** 0.76 is relative odds, so "24% less likely" is the authors' and press's wording; the upper CI (0.98)
  is close to 1, and the authors note missing outcome data and modest power. Measured on the hands only, in under-55s.
- **Contamination:** commentators note the discretionary group also used sunscreen, and the daily group did not
  always comply, so the trial likely underestimates the contrast; daily use was only ensured during 1992–96.
- **Sample size:** 1,621 in the papers; a Cochrane review notes the protocol reported 1,626. Age range given as
  25–74 or 25–75.
- **UV cameras:** they mainly show pigment (melanin) changes; a dermatologist quoted in coverage says they are not
  a marker of skin cancer. One frequently repeated "1.5–2 mm deep" claim (CURE, 2006) was not used: unverified.
- **Film thickness:** a lab test dose, not what trial participants applied; real-world application is usually
  thinner. "Silicone" as the cast material is assumed from the microtopography literature; confirm in Hughes'
  methods.

## What a dermatologist must check

- That no line reads as advice to use sunscreen, or as a product claim (none is named). The film describes one
  trial's evidence.
- That "about half, in small numbers" fairly represents HR 0.50 (0.24–1.02), and that the on-screen badge is enough.
- That "come out lower" for squamous cell cancers fairly represents the tumour-count result.
- That "about a quarter less likely to show increased ageing" fairly renders relative odds 0.76.
- That the UV-camera line ("damage the naked eye misses, building long before anything shows") and the takeaway's
  "record" metaphor are accurate, and that the UV view is clearly a model.
- That the model hands never imply a before/after of a real person.
- That the to-scale line ("at a lab's test dose") cannot be read as a recommended amount.
