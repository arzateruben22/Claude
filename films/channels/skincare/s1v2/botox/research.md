# What botulinum toxin does: research notes (s1v2)

Mechanism only. No advice, no product. Every claim is in `claims.json`; all were checked through search excerpts
(WebFetch is blocked: pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov does not resolve from this session). Needs a board-certified
dermatologist's sign-off before posting: this is a treatment topic.

## Naming: brand vs molecule

"Botox" is a brand name (onabotulinumtoxinA). The voice says "botulinum toxin" or "it"; the brand appears only in
the topic name and in source titles. The one product-specific number (20 units for frown lines, c8) is taken
from that product's label, because units are product-specific (one unit = that product's mouse LD50) and are not
interchangeable between brands. No product is named, shown or recommended on screen.

## Angles (one line each)

1. **It never touches the skin.** The target is a nerve ending: it cuts one protein so the "contract" message is
   made but never sent. (wrong idea: it fills or freezes skin)
2. **Sausage poison to medicine.** Justinus Kerner traced a deadly paralysis to spoiled sausages in the 1820s and
   guessed it might be a medicine; Alan Scott made the guess real for crossed eyes (1980); the Carruthers noticed
   the frown lines (1992). (a person)
3. **The dose is the whole story.** Among the most potent toxins known, lethal in estimated nanograms per kilo; a
   frown-line dose is well under a hundredth of the estimated lethal amount. (stakes)
4. **The nerve repairs itself.** It wears off because the nerve sprouts new endings, then the original junction
   recovers and the sprouts are pruned. (a self-repair story)

## Chosen angle and why it stops a scroll

Angle 1, carried by 2 and 3: "What does it switch off? Not the skin, but one message." Almost everyone has a
picture of what it does to a face; almost no one has a picture of the half-second message between a nerve and a
muscle. The hook names a thing the viewer thinks they know and the answer is not on the face. Kerner gives the why
a person and an irony (a sausage poison he guessed could heal); the potency gives the whoa real stakes without
hype; the regrowing nerve gives the takeaway its turn and explains why the effect is temporary. Scott and the
Carruthers live on the end-card footnote, not in the voice (Flow: name a person aloud only when the story turns
on them).

## Disputed or uncertain points

- **Lethal dose is an estimate, not a measurement.** Human figures are extrapolated from animal data and vary by
  route (IV/IM ≈ 1–2 ng/kg; inhaled ≈ 10–13 ng/kg; oral ≈ 1 µg/kg per Arnon) and by source (a 2006 snapshot of a
  secondary source gives 200–300 pg/kg). Badged "estimated"; the voice never says a number.
- **Units vs mass give different fractions.** Units: 20 ÷ ≈ 3,000 ≈ 0.7 %. Mass: 20 units ≈ 0.15 ng (at ≈ 0.73 ng
  per 100 units, a secondary source, clintox.org) against 90–150 ng ≈ 0.1–0.2 %. These do not fully agree
  (3,000 units by mass would be only ≈ 22 ng, below Arnon's range), likely because unit and mass estimates come
  from different species and assays. Both put a frown-line dose under 1 %, so the voice says only "well under a
  hundredth". The ≈ 0.73 ng/100 U figure was not confirmed on the label.
- **Monkey LD50.** The ≈ 39–40 U/kg primate figure (Scott & Suzuki 1988, Mov Disord) was confirmed only as a
  citation; the nonclinical label summary says monkeys tolerated ≤ 16 U/kg and showed signs and occasional deaths
  at ≥ 24 U/kg.
- **Why it wears off.** Sprouting then recovery of the parent terminal (de Paiva et al. 1999, PNAS) is mostly from
  rodent muscle. Reviews also debate how long the toxin's light chain stays active and how cleaved SNAP-25 is
  cleared; some argue sprouting is the main limit on duration, others that SNAP-25 turnover is. The voice says
  only "the nerve grows new endings"; the picture shows a sprout and then the original ending relighting.
- **Duration range.** AAD and the label: about 3–4 months; other sources 2–6 months, some reviews 4–6 months for
  therapeutic uses. Said "a few months".
- **Cleft width.** StatPearls ≈ 50 nm; other sources 20–60 nm, ≈ 60 nm (mouse). "About two thousand" holds for
  50 nm only; at 20 nm it would be 5,000. Badged "≈".
- **History details.** Kerner's case counts differ (76 + 155 vs 230 total); not voiced. Scott's 1980 paper is
  cited in two journals in different reference lists (Ophthalmology 87(10) vs J Pediatr Ophthalmol Strabismus
  17(1)); the end-card footnote gives only the year. The Carruthers' first frown-line report is 1992
  (J Dermatol Surg Oncol 18(1):17–21, 18 patients); some histories say 1996 or date the observation to 1987.
- **"Not the skin."** The toxin acts at the nerve-muscle junction; the skin flattens because the muscle under it
  pulls less. A dermatologist confirms this wording does not overstate it.

## What a dermatologist must check

1. That nothing reads as a recommendation, a safety reassurance or an endorsement: especially "well under a
   hundredth of the amount estimated to kill" (it must not read as "it's safe"; serious effects can occur far below
   a lethal dose, and the protocol says so).
2. The comparison in c8/c9 (units are product-specific; 20 units is one product's labelled glabellar dose).
3. "Not the skin, but one message" and "cuts one small protein the message needs to get out" as a fair
   simplification of SNAP-25 cleavage and blocked vesicle fusion.
4. "Silenced for a few months, until the nerve grows new endings" against current understanding of recovery in
   human facial muscle.
5. The lethal-dose badge and the on-screen figures (route, estimate, source).
6. Kerner's role and the end-card footnote (Scott 1980, Carruthers 1992).
7. Platform health-information rules (bible §9) for an injectable-treatment topic.
