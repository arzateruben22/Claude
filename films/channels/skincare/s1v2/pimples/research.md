# Research: how we get pimples (Outer Layer, s1v2)

All checks were done through web-search excerpts on 2026-11-02. Page fetches are blocked in this session
(www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov does not resolve through the proxy), so every source still needs a person to open it.
Skin School already covers "why a pore clogs" (`films/skin-school-tiktok/script.md`); this episode never
explains the plug.

## Strongest angles (one line each)

1. **The fight you never saw:** a red spot is the last visible scene of an immune response that started under
   skin that looked clear (Jeremy 2003; Do 2008).
2. **It isn't a germ that doesn't belong:** C. acnes lives in follicles with and without acne; the spot is
   largely the body's answer to it (Platsidaki & Dessinioti 2018; Huang 2024), which is debated.
3. **The wall gives way:** white cells flood in, the follicle wall can rupture into the dermis, and a squeeze
   often pushes contents deeper, where scars and dark marks are more likely (StatPearls; AAD).
4. **Pus is the defenders:** a whitehead's pus is mostly dead white cells, not "dirt" (MedlinePlus).

## Chosen angle, and why it stops a scroll

Angle 1, carrying 2 and 3 inside it: "Is this spot new?" Everyone has woken to a spot that seems to have come
from nowhere. The answer (no: the defence was already gathered under clear skin, and in one study more than a
quarter of new inflamed spots rose from skin that looked clear in photos) flips a daily experience. The why
corrects a wrong idea (it's an infection), and the whoa gives the stakes everyone has wondered about: what a squeeze
does inside the skin. It explains what happens, never what to do.

## Dropped or hedged

- **"A pimple starts forming 2–3 weeks (or 8 weeks, or 90 days) before you see it."** Dropped. Every version
  found traces to brands and clinic blogs (Curology itself says no one knows for sure how fast a pimple forms);
  no peer-reviewed measurement of the microcomedone-to-spot interval was found. The film says "long before" and
  shows no weeks. Do 2008's 2-week photo interval is a study design, not a formation time.
- **"Up to 95% of acne patients scar."** Dropped: traced to Layton 1994 via a 2017 review ("some degree of scarring
  ... up to 95%"); about half have clinically relevant scars; a Brazilian population study found 22%. Too
  variable to voice.
- **"85% of people aged 12–24 have acne."** Not used; found only on secondary sites (GPnotebook, a market report
  attributing it to the AAD).
- **C. acnes size (0.4–0.7 × 3–5 µm vs 0.8–0.9 µm in a type-strain record).** Not used: sources disagree.
- **Microcomedone size ("up to 0.4 mm" by reflectance microscopy).** Not used: one unattributed excerpt.

## Disputed or uncertain

- **Which comes first, inflammation or the plug?** Jeremy 2003 puts inflammation at the earliest stage; the
  traditional model puts the microcomedone first. The script says both began out of sight and takes no side on
  order.
- **The bacterium's role.** Reviews say the immune response, not the bacterium itself, is key; others stress
  phylotype imbalance, biofilms or a pro-/anti-inflammatory imbalance. Badged `debated`; "largely".
- **Microcomedones in clear skin:** Fontao 2020 harvested them from non-lesional skin; another study found none in
  uninvolved follicles; an IFSCC abstract found microcomedone features in 28% of normal-looking biopsies.
- **Rupture mechanism:** neutrophil enzymes "are thought to" cause it (hedged; badged `model`).
- **Do 2008's "normal skin":** normal in a photograph; tiny comedones or short-lived lesions between photos were
  missed (the authors' own limitation).

## What a dermatologist must check

- That "a squeeze often pushes it further, where a mark is more likely" reads as description, not advice, and
  matches the AAD's wording (`often`, `more likely`).
- That "What makes the spot is largely how your body answers it" is fair to current pathogenesis, with sebum,
  hormones and keratinisation not denied.
- That "white cells ... the kind that ends up as pus" is fair for acne pustules (neutrophil-rich).
- That "about a tenth of a paper's thickness" for a neutrophil (8–15 µm) is acceptable rounding.
- The Jeremy 2003 DOI (from the citation, not shown in a search excerpt) and the JPAD review's title and authors.
