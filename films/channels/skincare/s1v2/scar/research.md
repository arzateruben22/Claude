# What's in a scar: research

Checked 2026-11-02 through web-search excerpts only. Page fetches fail in this session
(pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov does not resolve), so every source still needs a person to open it.

## Angles (one line each)

1. **Patch, not reweave.** Normal dermis is a basket weave of collagen; a scar is the same protein laid in
   parallel bundles, with no hair follicles or sweat glands.
2. **Never as strong.** Even a fully mature scar tops out at about 80% of the strength of the skin around it
   (Levenson et al. 1965, rat skin, the classic tensile curve).
3. **Born scarless.** Until about 22–24 weeks of gestation, human fetal skin heals a wound by regenerating it,
   weave and follicles included; after that it scars.
4. **The paper line.** On most of the body, only the epidermis (about a paper's thickness) regrows perfectly;
   any injury into the dermis heals with a scar.

## The angle chosen

All four, as one thread: the weave (1) → the patch (1) → the cost (2) → the lost ability (3) → where the line
sits (4). The scroll-stopper is the pairing of 2 and 3: the "healed" skin you live with is a permanent
four-fifths-strength patch, and you once could heal without one. The hook, "Is a scar still skin?", is a
question everyone can look down and test on their own body, and the takeaway answers it.

## Disputed or uncertain

- **The 80% is a rat figure.** Levenson's curve is from rat skin. Surgical teaching applies "about 80%" to
  human skin, but the human data are thinner; the screen badges "rat skin · classic study" and the voice never
  says "your scar".
- **Timing varies by source.** Strength at 3 weeks is given as 20% or 30%; remodelling as "months" to "1–2
  years". The film avoids these.
- **Sweat glands are not always all gone.** A 2005 study (PubMed 15740645) found residual, disorganised sweat
  glands in some hypertrophic scars. "Leaves out the hair and the sweat glands" describes the usual case.
- **Fetal threshold.** 22–24 weeks in humans; it varies with wound size and site, and the mechanism "remains
  largely unknown". Evidence comes from fetal surgery, pathology and animal models (mouse E16–E18.5, sheep).
- **Epidermis-only wounds** usually heal without a scar, but not always (MedicineNet: "will not always produce a
  scar"). The voice says only that the top layer grows back without a seam.
- **The epidermis thickness source** is an anatomy atlas and an open textbook, not StatPearls (whose excerpt
  gave no numbers). Swap in a peer-reviewed figure if one is preferred.

## What a dermatologist must check

- That "most of your skin's strength is collagen" (meaning the dermis) and "all in one direction" are fair
  plain-English versions of the histology.
- That "about four-fifths of the original strength" is acceptable with the rat-study badge.
- That "until about the sixth month of pregnancy ... regrows the weave, follicles and all" is accurate and
  handled sensitively; the fetal section is an illustration, never a photo.
- That nothing reads as advice about scars or as a promise that shallow wounds never scar.
