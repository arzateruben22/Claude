# Research: what makes skin glow (Outer Layer s1v2)

session (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov failed to resolve through the proxy). A person opens each link before posting.

## Strongest angles (one line each)

1. **The glow comes from inside.** Only about 4–7 % of light bounces off the surface; most goes in, scatters, and
   a large share comes back out tinted by melanin and blood (Lister 2012; Jacques, OMLC).
2. **Mirror vs haze.** The same few-percent glint looks like a highlight when the surface is smooth and hydrated
   and like a dull haze when it's dry and rough (Dąbrowska 2016; Jiang & DeLaCruz 2011).
3. **Your face as a blood-colour readout.** People asked to make faces look healthy turned up oxygenated blood
   colour (Stephen et al. 2009); a flush is vessels widening (DermNet).
4. **Skin is optically glass.** Stratum corneum, n ≈ 1.55, reflects about what a window pane does; Fresnel's
   formula gives ≈ 4.7 % head-on (computed).

## Chosen angle, and why it stops a scroll

Angle 1, with 2 as the "normal/change" setup and 3 as the whoa. "Where does a glow come from?" has an obvious
wrong answer (the shiny surface) and a true one that flips it: the shine is the small part, and the glow is light
that has been inside you and come back carrying your blood's colour. The picture is one beam the camera follows in
and out, which suits the one-world, few-cuts rule, and the ending loops to frame 0.

## Disputed or uncertain

- **4–7 %:** the range is old (Scheuplein 1964) and passed down through reviews and patents; the Lister excerpt is
  cut off mid-sentence. It holds near head-on; at grazing angles reflection rises steeply (Fresnel), which is part
  of why foreheads and cheekbones catch highlights. The film says "a few percent" and badges "near head-on light".
- **1.55:** a commonly cited dry value; reported values span 1.45–1.55 (1.53 and 1.54 also appear). Hydration
  lowers the index mismatch. On screen as "≈ 1.55".
- **40 % back out:** a single worked example (one wavelength, polarization set-up) from Jacques's notes, not a
  general constant. More melanin absorbs more, so the share coming back is smaller in deeply pigmented skin and in
  blue light. "Mostly from within" (takeaway) leans on this; badged "one example (model)".
- **Hydration → gloss:** the peer-reviewed link is hydration → smoother surface (single-participant pilot) and
  hydration → less surface scattering, more translucency (one industry study). A direct, measured hydration →
  specular-gloss number was not found. Skin gloss is also driven by sebum (oiliness), which the film leaves out.
  A Japanese study found perceived transparency tracked diffuse, not specular, reflection.
- **Penetration depths** vary with skin type, site, beam width and model; values from ≈ 0.3–2 mm (400–700 nm) to
  4–5 mm for red appear. On screen as "estimate".
- **Stephen et al. 2009** measures perceived health from photos, not actual health; the film says only that people
  turned the colour up.

## What a dermatologist must check

- That "a rush of blood sends it back redder" and the face-colour panel read as optics, not as a statement about
  rosacea, flushing disorders or any condition, and not as advice.
- That "mostly from within" is fair across skin tones (higher melanin → more absorption), and that the film shows
  several tones, never one alone.
- That "dry it out, and it scatters light" doesn't read as a dry-skin or moisturiser claim (no product, no advice).
- The 4–7 %, 1.55 and depth figures against the original papers (Lister 2012; Scheuplein 1964; Ash 2017).
