The fight you never saw | Skin Sense No. 001

**Description**

```
Is this spot new? Its fight began under skin that looked perfectly clear.

It's easy to picture an infection. But it's the most common bacterium in clear, oily skin, and skin with acne carries no more. What makes the spot is largely your body's answer. Is it new? No: a fight begun out of sight, against a germ that even helps build your skin's barrier.

Series: Season 1: Under the surface, part 1 of 6. Next: where the light on your face goes

Sources
1. Acne lesions begin as a microcomedone, a subclinical (not visible) change in the follicle that precedes visible comedones and inflammatory lesions; microcomedones can be found in acne patients' skin that looks normal. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.1111/jdv.15926
2. In a study tracking facial acne lesions by aligned photographs (25 untreated people, every 2 weeks for 12 weeks), 28% of inflammatory lesions were preceded by normal-appearing skin. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaad.2007.12.024
3. Biopsies of clinically uninvolved skin of acne patients showed raised perifollicular T cells (not to papule levels), macrophage numbers similar to papules, and upregulated IL-1: inflammation is involved at the earliest stage of lesion development. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1523-1747.2003.12321.x
4. Cutibacterium acnes is the most abundant bacterium living in healthy, sebum-rich (oily) human skin sites such as the face and back. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.3390/microorganisms9030628
5. Cutibacterium acnes is a commensal of the pilosebaceous follicle, found in both normal and acne skin; reviews report no quantitative difference between acne patients and controls. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.15659.1
6. Current reviews hold that the host immune response to C. acnes, rather than the bacterium itself, plays a key role in acne inflammation; this is debated. — frontiersin.org: https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fimmu.2024.1275269/full
7. Neutrophils are recruited into the follicle and ingest C. acnes; the hydrolytic enzymes they release are thought to weaken and rupture the follicular wall. — jpad.com.pk: https://jpad.com.pk/index.php/jpad/article/download/398/373/804
8. Follicles can rupture, releasing bacteria, keratin and proinflammatory lipids into the surrounding dermis, which worsens inflammation. — NCBI (NIH): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK459173/
9. Human bone marrow releases roughly 5-10 x 10^10 neutrophils (white cells) a day at baseline; with about 8.2 billion people alive, that is about 6-12 neutrophils per person on Earth, every day ('several'). — NIH: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2930213/
10. Pus is a collection of dead tissue, dead bacteria and live and dead phagocytes (white cells such as neutrophils). — medlineplus.gov: https://medlineplus.gov/ency/imagepages/9478.htm
11. A resting human neutrophil is a sphere about 8 µm across (volume ≈ 299 µm³); spread on a blood smear it measures about 12–15 µm. Against ≈ 100 µm paper that is about a twelfth to an eighth: ten cells ≈ 80 µm, short of 100 µm: 'ten in a row still wouldn't span'. — scholars.duke.edu: https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/790236
12. Commensal C. acnes, alone among common skin bacteria tested, increased lipid synthesis by epidermal keratinocytes (about threefold: ceramides, cholesterol, free fatty acids, triglycerides), driven mainly by the short-chain fatty acid propionic acid via PPARalpha; the extra lipids improved barrier measures (antimicrobial activity, paracellular diffusion, transepidermal water loss). Shown in cultured cells and mouse skin, with a matching signal in human acne skin. — doi.org: https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adg6262
```
