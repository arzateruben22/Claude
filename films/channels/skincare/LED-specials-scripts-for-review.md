## Episode 7 · "LED light therapy, explained"

*What it covers:* what LED therapy is; how blue, red and near-infrared light act at different depths; the other advertised colours (amber, green, cyan, purple, white) and how much evidence each has; why dose, schedule and device matter more than colour; realistic expectations. Captions show numbers as numerals.

**hook** — *three beams fall onto the glowing voxel skin from above: blue stops near the surface, red goes deeper, a dotted ghost beam (near-infrared, labelled "invisible, shown as dots") goes deepest*  
Every LED colour promises something different. So what does the light actually do, once it reaches your skin?

**what** — *inside one skin cell: light arrives and a small molecule in a mitochondrion glows teal*  
LED therapy shines low-power light onto the skin. [1] Some molecules in your cells absorb certain wavelengths, and that can change what the cell does. [2] Scientists call it photobiomodulation. [3]

**depth** — *a side cut through the skin; the three beams stop at their depths; a nanometre ruler along the top*  
The wavelength decides how deep the light goes. Blue works near the surface. Red and near-infrared reach deeper. [4]

**blue** — *inside one pore: bacteria absorb the blue light and tiny sparks of reactive oxygen appear; one word on screen: "415 nm"*  
Blue light, around four hundred and fifteen nanometres, is absorbed by pigments that acne bacteria make inside the pore. [5] That releases reactive oxygen, which harms the bacteria. [5] Dermatologists use it for inflamed pimples, but reviews rate the evidence as low quality. [6,7] And visible light doesn't clear blackheads, whiteheads or cysts. [7]

**red** — *deeper, a fibroblast: red light reaches it, its mitochondria brighten, and new collagen fibres slowly weave*  
Red light, around six hundred and thirty to six hundred and sixty nanometres, is thought to act on the mitochondria, the cell's power stations. [8] In small controlled trials, red and near-infrared light improved fine lines, roughness and collagen over several weeks. [9,10]

**nir** — *the dotted near-infrared beam passes deepest, under the red; label "invisible, shown as dots"*  
Near-infrared is invisible to your eyes, and it reaches deeper still. [11,4] It's studied for tissue recovery and for calming inflammation. [3]

**colours** — *a spectrum strip; amber, green, cyan, purple and white chips light one by one, each with an honesty label: "small studies", "little evidence", "a mix of two"*  
Then there are the extra colours. Amber, near five hundred and ninety nanometres, has a few small studies for fine lines and redness. [12,13] Green, cyan and white are marketed for tone, calm or everything at once, but we found little clinical evidence for them. [14] Purple is usually red and blue switched on together. [15]

**dose** — *three dials (distance, minutes, sessions); the same red beam brightens and dims; a curve rises, peaks, then falls*  
Colour isn't the dose. Results depend on the wavelength, how much light reaches the skin, for how long, how often, and the device itself. [16] And more isn't always better: past a point, extra light can do less. [17]

**cta** — *the beams settle; like/follow moment*  
If you're feeling a little calmer, tap like and follow.

**close** — *back to frame 0, softer; a calendar of weeks fills one square at a time; final hold ≥ 1.5 s*  
In the studies, people came back a few times a week, for a month or more. [18] So any change is gradual. Light works slowly, with steady use.

   *Checks:* Light therapies of this kind use relatively low fluences (0.04-50 J/cm2) and power densities (under 100 mW/cm2).

   *Checks:* The primary site of red/near-infrared light absorption is thought to be the mitochondria, specifically cytochrome c oxidase; the mechanism is a hypothesis.

   *Checks:* In a randomised, sham-controlled split-face trial (76 people), 633 nm, 830 nm and combined LED twice a week for four weeks reduced wrinkles (best case 36%) and raised elasticity.
11. NASA Science, Infrared Waves. [link](https://science.nasa.gov/ems/07_infraredwaves/)

12. Weiss RA et al., Clinical trial of a novel non-thermal LED array for reversal of photoaging. Lasers Surg Med 2005. [link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15654716/)  
   *Checks:* An uncontrolled prospective study of 90 people treated with a 590 nm LED array reported smoother texture, fewer fine lines and less redness and pigmentation.
13. Mota LR et al., Photobiomodulation Reduces Periocular Wrinkle Volume by 30%: A Randomized Controlled Trial. Photobiomodul Photomed Laser Surg 2023. [link](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36780572/)  
   *Checks:* A split-face randomised trial in 137 women found red (660 nm) and amber (590 nm) light each reduced eye-area wrinkle volume by about 30% after 10 sessions; hydration and elasticity did not change.

   *Checks:* Our PubMed search (12 Nov 2026) found no controlled trials of green or cyan LED light for skin appearance; the 2016 Cochrane acne review covers blue, red, yellow and infrared light; in one acne trial (Papageorgiou 2000), cool white light was the control and did worst.

   *Checks:* Device makers describe 'purple' modes as red and blue LEDs lit together.

## Episode 8 · "Celluma PRO LED panel, explained"

*What it covers:* Celluma PRO's three wavelengths and programs; exactly what its FDA 510(k) clearances cover (K152280, K171323); "cleared" vs "approved"; the flexible panel around a faceless head-and-neck form, an arm and the back; a fair comparison with Omnilux Contour Face, CurrentBody Series 2, LightStim for Wrinkles and Dermalux Flex MD; no head-to-head study exists, so no device is called best. Disclosure on screen for the first 3 seconds and in the post.

**hook** — *a flexible LED panel, drawn in code, bends in a slow arc around a smooth, featureless head-and-neck form in the episode's one skin tone; small disclosure line for 3 s*  
This light therapy panel bends. [1] So what does it do, what is it cleared for, and how does it compare?

**what** — *the panel's three LED colours light in turn, with labels 465, 640 and 880 nm; the last is drawn as dots, "invisible"*  
The Celluma PRO uses three wavelengths: blue at four hundred and sixty-five nanometres, red at six hundred and forty, and invisible near-infrared at eight hundred and eighty. [2] Blue aims at acne bacteria in the pore. Red and near-infrared aim at the cells beneath.

**programs** — *three program chips (Acne, Wrinkles, Aches & Pains) and a 30-minute timer ring*  
It has three programs, Acne, Wrinkles, and Aches and Pains, and each one runs for thirty minutes. [3]

**fda** — *a clean card: "FDA 510(k) clearances K152280, K171323", then three icons appear: a pore, a fine line, a joint*  
Its FDA clearances cover blue light for mild to moderate inflammatory acne, [4] visible and infrared light for full-face wrinkles, [5] and infrared heat for temporary relief of minor muscle and joint pain. [4] Cleared means the FDA found it substantially equivalent to devices already sold. It isn't the same as approved. [6]

**fit** — *the panel curves around the faceless head and neck, then a forearm, then the upper back, hands-free; a small "1 inch" gap marker*  
The panel is flexible. It curves around the face and neck, an arm or the back, without being held, about an inch or less from bare skin. [1] That matters, because results depend on how much light actually reaches the skin. [7]

**compare** — *five device cards side by side (Celluma PRO, Omnilux Contour Face, CurrentBody Series 2, LightStim for Wrinkles, Dermalux Flex MD); rows light up: wavelengths, time, fit*  
How does it compare? The Omnilux Contour Face and CurrentBody Series 2 masks use red and near-infrared for ten minutes. [8,9] LightStim for Wrinkles is handheld, three minutes per area. [10] Dermalux Flex MD is another flexible panel, with blue, red and near-infrared. [11]

**dose** — *a "published output" row: values on some cards, "not published" on others; then a row of K-numbers, one per device*  
Wavelength alone doesn't tell you the dose. [7] Brands publish their output in different ways, and the dose at the skin isn't published for the PRO. [12] Each of these devices has its own FDA clearance, and a clearance belongs to a specific model. [13]

**evidence** — *a small stack of study cards; the top card reads "head-to-head: none found"*  
The research is still thin. We found no study comparing Celluma with another LED device, and the published Celluma studies are small. [14,15] So no device here has shown it beats the rest.

**cta** — *the panel settles into its curve; like/follow moment*  
If you're feeling a little calmer, tap like and follow.

**close** — *back to frame 0's arc, softer; a calendar of four weeks fills three squares a week; final hold ≥ 1.5 s*  
Its instructions describe thirty-minute sessions, about three times a week, for four weeks. [16] Any change builds gradually, with steady use.
