## hook {shot S01: three beams fall onto the glowing voxel skin from above: blue stops near the surface, red goes deeper, a dotted ghost beam (near-infrared, labelled "invisible, shown as dots") goes deepest · pre: 0.3 · energy: 0.4}

## what {shot S02: inside one skin cell: light arrives and a small molecule in a mitochondrion glows teal · energy: 0.4}
vo: LED therapy shines low-power light onto the skin. {c1} Some molecules in your cells absorb certain wavelengths, and that can change what the cell does. {c2} Scientists call it photobiomodulation. {c3}

## depth {shot S03: a side cut through the skin; the three beams stop at their depths; a nanometre ruler along the top · energy: 0.45}
vo: The wavelength decides how deep the light goes. {-} Blue works near the surface. Red and near-infrared reach deeper. {c4}

## blue {shot S04: inside one pore: bacteria absorb the blue light and tiny sparks of reactive oxygen appear; one word on screen: "415 nm" · energy: 0.5}
vo: Blue light, around four hundred and fifteen nanometres, is absorbed by pigments that acne bacteria make inside the pore. {c5} That releases reactive oxygen, which harms the bacteria. {c5} Dermatologists use it for inflamed pimples, but reviews rate the evidence as low quality. {c6}{c7} And visible light doesn't clear blackheads, whiteheads or cysts. {c7}

## red {shot S05: deeper, a fibroblast: red light reaches it, its mitochondria brighten, and new collagen fibres slowly weave · energy: 0.5}
vo: Red light, around six hundred and thirty to six hundred and sixty nanometres, is thought to act on the mitochondria, the cell's power stations. {c8} In small controlled trials, red and near-infrared light improved fine lines, roughness and collagen over several weeks. {c9}{c10}

## nir {shot S06: the dotted near-infrared beam passes deepest, under the red; label "invisible, shown as dots" · energy: 0.45}
vo: Near-infrared is invisible to your eyes, and it reaches deeper still. {c11}{c4} It's studied for tissue recovery and for calming inflammation. {c3}

## colours {shot S07: a spectrum strip; amber, green, cyan, purple and white chips light one by one, each with an honesty label: "small studies", "little evidence", "a mix of two" · energy: 0.45}
vo: Then there are the extra colours. {-} Amber, near five hundred and ninety nanometres, has a few small studies for fine lines and redness. {c12}{c13} Green, cyan and white are marketed for tone, calm or everything at once, but we found little clinical evidence for them. {c14} Purple is usually red and blue switched on together. {c15}

## dose {shot S08: three dials (distance, minutes, sessions); the same red beam brightens and dims; a curve rises, peaks, then falls · energy: 0.5}
vo: Colour isn't the dose. {-} Results depend on the wavelength, how much light reaches the skin, for how long, how often, and the device itself. {c16} And more isn't always better: past a point, extra light can do less. {c17}

## cta {shot S09: the beams settle; like/follow moment · energy: 0.4}
vo: If you're feeling a little calmer, tap like and follow. {-}

## close {shot S10: back to frame 0, softer; a calendar of weeks fills one square at a time; final hold ≥ 1.5 s · energy: 0.3}
vo: In the studies, people came back a few times a week, for a month or more. {c18} So any change is gradual. Light works slowly, with steady use. {-}
