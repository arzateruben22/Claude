# Possibility Lab · Season 3 · Episode 3: "What If It Got Colder Every Second and Couldn't Stop?" (script v1; fact-check before any render)

About 9 min, 16:9 (2560x1440), for YouTube's main feed, narrated by a synthetic voice for now (the owner will record it), with an original score. A thought experiment:
the temperature around you starts at a pleasant twenty degrees and falls, a little more every second, and nothing can stop it. You wear an imaginary suit that keeps you alive, so the film is
about what the cold does to the WORLD. Every number comes from `calc.py` (boiling and freezing points at one atmosphere); `claims.json` ties each sentence to a source or marks it a derivation.

The picture: you in a street scene that slowly frosts over, over your shoulder and at your eye line; ONE big live number is always on screen, the temperature (degrees Celsius, with kelvin
and degrees Fahrenheit under it), falling from 20 degrees to just above absolute zero and never resetting. Each milestone is a beat where something visibly changes: water, dry ice, liquid oxygen,
a rain of liquid air, the last gas, and the floor that can never be reached. The ending is "wait, what?": even at the coldest possible temperature, nothing fully stops.
House rules: one text element per idea; the caption carries the sentence; no voiceless gap over 1.2 s; motion every 2 to 3 s; badge "Thought experiment" throughout; the human parts are hedged and never graphic.

One line per beat. `*starred*` words get a camera landing or a sound hit. `{c1,c2}` after a sentence ties it to `claims.json`; `{-}` marks a sentence that states nothing outside the film.

vo: What if the temperature around you started to fall, a little more every second, and nothing in the universe could stop it? {-} You would watch the world freeze, then freeze again, and then something stranger than freezing. {-} By the end, even the air would turn to rain. {c8}

## rules {shot: a thermometer-like column beside you; kelvin and Fahrenheit under the big number · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: Here are the rules. {-} The cold arrives everywhere at once, and it only ever gets colder. {-} You are wearing an imaginary suit, so you survive. {-} The film is about what happens to everything else. {-} On screen is one number, the temperature in degrees Celsius, and under it the same temperature in kelvin, the scale that starts at the coldest possible temperature. {c1} Nothing resets. {-}

## zero {shot: puddles skin over with ice, rain turns to snow, the temperature passes 0 °C · milestone: zero degrees · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: At zero degrees Celsius, water freezes. {c2} Puddles turn to ice, and rain turns to snow. {-} That is the temperature everyone knows. {-} It is also only the beginning, and a very mild beginning: you have fallen just twenty degrees. {c1}

## sea {shot: the sea surface skins over; a white ice crust spreads out from the shore; the thermometer passes -2 °C · milestone: the sea freezes · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: The sea is a little more stubborn, because salt lowers its freezing point to about minus two degrees. {c3} But it is only a delay. {-} A crust of ice spreads across the oceans from the shore, and the water underneath is sealed away from the sky. {-} For a while, it would stay liquid below, kept warm by its own depth. {-}

## depth {shot: a cutaway: ice above, dark liquid ocean below, a faint glow far down at the sea floor · milestone: the ocean under the ice · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: Under the ice, the oceans would not freeze quickly. {-} Ice is a good insulator, and the sea floor is warmed from below by the Earth's own heat. {c17} Life that depends on sunlight would not survive, but the deep water could stay liquid for a very long time. {c17} So the first thing to give up is the sky, not the sea. {-}

## forty {shot: two thermometers, Celsius and Fahrenheit, converge on the same number · milestone: minus forty · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: At minus forty degrees, something happens that happens nowhere else on the scale. {-} Celsius and Fahrenheit agree. {c4} Minus forty is minus forty on both. {c4} It is a very cold day in the far north of Canada or Siberia, and people who live there still go to work. {-}

## dry {shot: the clouds thin and vanish; the sky turns a hard clear blue; frost feathers grow on the glass · milestone: the sky empties of water · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: By minus sixty, the sky has gone strangely clear. {-} Cold air can hold almost no water vapour, so the clouds fall out as snow and frost, and they do not come back. {c18} The world turns sharp and silent, a blue dome over a white street. {-} Even the rubber on the cars goes stiff, and many metals turn brittle, and things that bent for a hundred years begin to crack. {c19}

## co2 {shot: the street signs grow white feathers of frost; a white powder forms on the road · milestone: dry ice · energy: 0.65} (__)
vo: At minus seventy-eight and a half, pure carbon dioxide turns straight from gas to solid, the white stuff we call dry ice. {c5} In real air, carbon dioxide is only a trace, so it needs it to be much colder, about one hundred and forty below. {c6} But when it does, the air itself starts to lose a gas, and a frost of dry ice settles on every surface. {c6} {-}

## record {shot: a weather-station thermometer on the ice: the coldest number ever measured flashes past · milestone: the coldest day on Earth · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: Somewhere on the way, you pass the coldest temperature ever measured on Earth. {-} It was minus eighty-nine point two degrees, at the Vostok station in Antarctica, in nineteen eighty-three. {c7} Everything colder than this is, for our planet, a number no one has ever felt. {c7}

## oxygen {shot: the air is clear; the street lamp bulbs glow; pale blue liquid begins to drip from the roof edges · milestone: oxygen turns liquid · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: At minus one hundred and eighty-three, oxygen turns to liquid. {c8} It is pale blue, and it is the gas that you breathe. {c8} It starts to pool on every surface that is colder than the air. {-} Argon follows just three degrees later. {c8} The air is still air, but the first of its ingredients is leaving. {-}

## nitrogen {shot: the sky itself drips: a cold liquid film spreads over the ground and the sea; the thermometer passes -196 °C · milestone: the air rains out · energy: 0.9} (__)
vo: At minus one hundred and ninety-six degrees, nitrogen turns to liquid, and nitrogen is almost four fifths of the air. {c8} {c9} Now the sky has a problem. {-} The air itself starts to rain out. {-} If all of it condensed, it would cover the whole planet with a layer of liquid air about twelve meters deep. {c10} The sky above would be nearly empty, and the sound of everything would disappear with it. {-}

## hydrogen {shot: the liquid layer begins to freeze; wisps of the last gases drift upward; the thermometer drops through -253 °C and toward -269 °C · milestone: the last gases · energy: 0.8} (__)
vo: A few tiny traces of gas are left. {-} Hydrogen turns to liquid at minus two hundred and fifty-three. {c8} Helium, the lightest and most stubborn of all, holds out to minus two hundred and sixty-nine, only four degrees above the coldest temperature there can be. {c8} {c11} After that, there is no gas left in the air at all. {-}

## strange {shot: a droplet of liquid helium climbs out of a cup, up the wall; a ring of current circles a frozen coil · milestone: the strange cold · energy: 0.85} (__)
vo: And now the cold itself starts to behave oddly. {-} Below about two kelvin, helium becomes a superfluid, a liquid with no friction at all. {c20} It creeps up the walls of its container and spills over the rim, as if gravity had stopped counting. {c20} Cool a ring of metal like mercury to about four kelvin, and an electric current can circle inside it for years without fading. {c21} The closer to the floor you go, the less like ordinary matter anything is. {-}

## scale {shot: the thermometer column zooms out: the coldest day on Earth sits near the top, a huge empty stretch below · milestone: how much scale is left · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: Step back and look at the whole scale. {-} Room temperature is about two hundred and ninety-three kelvin. {c1} The coldest day ever measured on Earth was about one hundred and eighty-four. {c7} So the entire range of weather, from a warm room to Antarctica at its worst, covers only about a third of the way down. {c1} {c7} Everything from there to the floor, nearly two thirds of the whole scale, is a place no planet has ever been. {c1}

## nebula {shot: a deep-space view: the Boomerang Nebula glows; the thermometer beside it reads 1 K · milestone: the coldest place in space · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: To find anywhere colder than this, you have to leave the Earth. {-} The Boomerang Nebula, five thousand light years away, is about one degree above the coldest possible temperature, the coldest natural place we know of. {c12} Even in space, the glow of the Big Bang is warmer than that, at about two point seven degrees. {c13} {c12}

## lab {shot: a laboratory with a glowing cloud of atoms in a trap; the thermometer reads picokelvin · milestone: the coldest anything has ever been · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: The coldest temperature anyone has ever reached is not in space. {-} It was made in a laboratory, with a cloud of atoms held in place by lasers and magnets. {c14} It was thirty-eight trillionths of a degree above the coldest possible temperature. {c14} That is far colder than the nebula, and far colder than anywhere else in the whole universe, as far as we know. {-}

## zero-point {shot: the thermometer reaches 0 K and the number stops just above it; the scale breaks into a staircase that never lands · milestone: the floor you cannot reach · energy: 0.95} (__)
vo: And the temperature never gets there. {-} The coldest possible temperature is minus two hundred and seventy-three point one five degrees, which we call absolute zero. {c1} The law of physics, the third law of thermodynamics, says that no machine, no trick and no amount of time can ever bring anything exactly to it. {c15} You can get closer, and closer, and closer, but each step needs more effort than the last. {c15} The number on screen can fall forever. {-} It can never land. {-}

## wait {shot: a single atom in a trap, still jittering: a small gold shimmer around it · milestone: wait, what? · energy: 0.9} (__)
vo: Now, the strangest part. {-} You might think that at absolute zero, everything would stop moving. {-} It does not. {-} Quantum physics says that even the coldest atom keeps a tiny, unavoidable jiggle, called zero-point motion. {c16} Nothing in the universe can ever be perfectly still. {c16}

## why {shot: the street: warm and bright again; a glass of water beside you · milestone: the real world · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: In the real world, none of this could happen. {-} The Earth is kept warm by the Sun, and the Sun would have to go out for a very long time. {-} Even then, the planet's own heat from deep inside would keep the oceans liquid underneath for a very long while. {c17} The cold is only a thought experiment. {-}
