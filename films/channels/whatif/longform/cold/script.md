# Possibility Lab · Season 3 · Episode 3: "What If It Got Colder Every Second and Couldn't Stop?" (script v1; fact-check before any render)

About 9 min, 16:9 (2560x1440), for YouTube's main feed, narrated by a synthetic voice for now (the owner will record it), with an original score. A thought experiment:
the temperature around you starts at a pleasant twenty degrees and falls, a little more every second, and nothing can stop it. You wear an imaginary suit that keeps you alive, so the film is
about what the cold does to the WORLD. Every number comes from `calc.py` (boiling and freezing points at one atmosphere); `claims.json` ties each sentence to a source or marks it a derivation.

The picture: you in a street scene that slowly frosts over, over your shoulder and at your eye line; ONE big live number is always on screen, the temperature (degrees Celsius, with kelvin
and degrees Fahrenheit under it), falling from 20 degrees to just above absolute zero and never resetting. Each milestone is a beat where something visibly changes: water, dry ice, liquid oxygen,
a rain of liquid air, the last gas, and the floor that can never be reached. The ending is "wait, what?": even at the coldest possible temperature, nothing fully stops.
House rules: one text element per idea; the caption carries the sentence; no voiceless gap over 1.2 s; motion every 2 to 3 s; badge "Thought experiment" throughout; the human parts are hedged and never graphic.

One line per beat. `*starred*` words get a camera landing or a sound hit. `{c1,c2}` after a sentence ties it to `claims.json`; `{-}` marks a sentence that states nothing outside the film.

## hook {shot: you in a calm street at dusk; the temperature reads 20 °C; frost begins to creep along the window ledge · badge: thought experiment · title: "What If It Got Colder Every Second and Couldn't Stop?" · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: What if the temperature around you started to fall, a little more every second, and nothing in the universe could stop it? {-} You would watch the world freeze, then freeze again, and then something stranger than freezing. {-} By the end, even the air would turn to rain. {c22}

## rules {shot: a thermometer-like column beside you; kelvin and Fahrenheit under the big number · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: Here are the rules. {-} The air everywhere gets colder, and it only ever gets colder. {-} You are wearing an imaginary suit, so you survive. {-} The film is about what happens to everything else. {-} On screen is one number, the temperature in degrees Celsius, and under it the same temperature in kelvin, the scale that starts at the coldest possible temperature. {c1} Nothing resets. {-}

## zero {shot: puddles skin over with ice, rain turns to snow, the temperature passes 0 °C · milestone: zero degrees · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: At zero degrees Celsius, water freezes. {c2} Puddles turn to ice, and rain turns to snow. {-} That is the temperature everyone knows. {-} It is also only the beginning, and a very mild beginning: you have fallen just twenty degrees. {c1}

## sea {shot: the sea surface skins over; a white ice crust spreads out from the shore; the thermometer passes -2 °C · milestone: the sea freezes · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: The sea is a little more stubborn, because salt lowers its freezing point to about minus two degrees. {c3} But it is only a delay. {-} A crust of ice spreads across the oceans from the shore, and the water underneath is sealed away from the sky. {-} For a while, it would stay liquid below, kept warm by its own depth. {-}

## depth {shot: a cutaway: a thin ice lid above, dark liquid ocean below, a faint glow far down at the sea floor · milestone: the ocean under the ice · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: Under the ice, the oceans would not freeze quickly. {-} In the minutes of this film, the ice lid is only centimetres thick, and a lid of ice slows the cold down. {c17} Without the Sun, my rough estimate is that it would take tens of thousands of years for the oceans to freeze all the way through. {c17} So the first thing to give up is the sky, not the sea. {-}

## forty {shot: two thermometers, Celsius and Fahrenheit, converge on the same number · milestone: minus forty · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: At minus forty degrees, something happens that happens nowhere else on the scale. {-} Celsius and Fahrenheit agree. {c4} Minus forty is minus forty on both. {c4} It is the one number where you never need to ask which scale someone means. {-}

## dry {shot: the clouds thin and vanish; the sky turns a hard clear blue; frost feathers grow on the glass · milestone: the sky empties of water · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: By minus sixty, the sky has gone strangely clear. {-} Cold air can hold almost no water vapour, so the clouds fall out as snow and frost, and they do not come back. {c18} The world turns sharp and silent, a blue dome over a white street. {-} Rubber goes stiff, and some metals, like ordinary steel, turn brittle, and things that bent for a hundred years begin to crack. {c19}

## record {shot: a weather-station thermometer on the ice: the coldest number ever measured flashes past · milestone: the coldest weather on Earth · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: Somewhere on the way, you pass the coldest air temperature ever measured at a weather station. {-} It was minus eighty-nine point two degrees, at the Vostok station in Antarctica, in nineteen eighty-three. {c7} Satellites have read about minus ninety-eight at the snow surface, but nothing colder than that has ever been recorded as weather. {c7} From here on, the cold is colder than any weather Earth has had. {-}

## co2 {shot: the street signs grow white feathers of frost; a white powder forms on the road as the number passes minus 140 · milestone: dry ice frost · energy: 0.65} (__)
vo: A jar of pure carbon dioxide turns from gas to solid at minus seventy-eight and a half, the white stuff we call dry ice. {c5} But in real air, carbon dioxide is only a trace, so the air has to get far colder, about one hundred and forty below. {c6} Then a frost of dry ice settles on every surface, and the air loses its first gas. {c6}

## oxygen {shot: the air is clear; the street lamp bulbs glow; the first pale blue drops form on the roof edges as the number passes -191 · milestone: the air starts to condense · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: A jar of pure oxygen boils at minus one hundred and eighty-three. {c8} But air is a mixture, and it holds out a little longer. {-} At about minus one hundred and ninety-one, the first drops begin to form, and they are rich in oxygen, so they are pale blue. {c22} It is the gas that you breathe, starting to pool on every surface that is colder than the air. {-}

## nitrogen {shot: the sky itself drips: a cold liquid film spreads over the ground and the sea; the thermometer passes -196 °C, then -203 °C, then -210 °C · milestone: the air rains out · energy: 0.9} (__)
vo: Nitrogen is almost four fifths of the air. {c9} By minus one hundred and ninety-four, the air has become liquid. {c22} But the sky does not empty all at once. {-} At minus one hundred and ninety-six, about four fifths of it is still gas. {c23} At minus two hundred and three, about a third. {c23} By minus two hundred and twenty-three, less than one percent. {c23} The air rains out, and it keeps raining as it gets colder. {-} If all of it came down, it would cover the planet with a layer of liquid air about twelve meters deep. {c10}

## hydrogen {shot: the liquid layer begins to freeze; wisps of the last gases drift upward; the thermometer drops toward -260 °C and below · milestone: the last gases · energy: 0.8} (__)
vo: A few tiny traces of gas are left. {-} There is so little of each that they hold out far longer than a jar of the pure gas would. {-} Neon lasts to about twelve kelvin, hydrogen to about six. {c24} Helium, the lightest and most stubborn, is a thousandth of a percent of the air and does not freeze out until below one kelvin. {c24} After that, there is no gas left in the air at all. {-}

## strange {shot: a droplet of liquid helium climbs out of a cup, up the wall; a ring of current circles a frozen coil · milestone: the strange cold · energy: 0.85} (__)
vo: And now the cold itself starts to behave oddly. {-} Below about two kelvin, helium becomes a superfluid, a liquid with no friction at all. {c20} It creeps up the walls of its container and spills over the rim. {c20} Cool a ring of lead below about seven kelvin, and an electric current can circle inside it for years without fading. {c21} The closer to the floor you go, the less like ordinary matter anything is. {-}

## scale {shot: the thermometer column zooms out: the coldest weather on Earth sits near the top, a huge empty stretch below · milestone: how much scale is left · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: Step back and look at the whole scale. {-} Room temperature is about two hundred and ninety-three kelvin. {c1} The coldest air ever measured on Earth was about one hundred and eighty-four. {c7} So the entire range of weather, from a warm room to Antarctica at its worst, covers just over a third of the way down. {c1} Everything from there to the floor, nearly two thirds of the whole scale, is colder than any weather Earth has recorded. {c1}

## nebula {shot: a deep-space view: the Boomerang Nebula glows; the thermometer beside it reads 1 K · milestone: the coldest natural place · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: To find a natural place this cold, you have to leave the Earth. {-} The Boomerang Nebula, five thousand light years away, is about one degree above the coldest possible temperature, the coldest natural place we know of. {c12} Even the glow left over from the Big Bang is warmer than that, at about two point seven degrees. {c13}

## lab {shot: a drop tower and a glowing cloud of atoms that spreads slowly; the thermometer reads picokelvin · milestone: the coldest anything has ever been · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: The coldest anything has ever been made is not in space. {-} It was in a laboratory. {-} A cloud of atoms, dropped down a tower and slowed with magnetic fields, moved as slowly as if it were thirty-eight trillionths of a degree above absolute zero. {c14} That is far colder than the nebula, and colder than anything else we know of. {-}

## zero-point {shot: the thermometer reaches 0 K and the number stops just above it; the scale breaks into a staircase that never lands · milestone: the floor you cannot reach · energy: 0.95} (__)
vo: And the temperature never gets there. {-} The coldest possible temperature is minus two hundred and seventy-three point one five degrees, which we call absolute zero. {c1} A law of physics, the third law of thermodynamics, says that nothing can be brought exactly to it in any finite number of steps. {c15} You can get closer, and closer, and closer, but each step needs more effort than the last. {c15} The number on screen can fall forever. {-} It can never land. {-}

## wait {shot: a single atom in a trap, still jittering: a small gold shimmer around it · milestone: wait, what? · energy: 0.9} (__)
vo: Now, the strangest part. {-} You might think that at absolute zero, everything would stop moving. {-} It does not. {-} Quantum physics says that even the coldest atom keeps a tiny, unavoidable jiggle, called zero-point motion. {c16} Nothing in the universe can ever be perfectly still. {c16}

## why {shot: the street: warm and bright again; a glass of water beside you · milestone: the real world · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: In the real world, none of this could happen. {-} The Earth is kept warm by the Sun, and the Sun would have to go out. {-} Even then, the planet's own heat would keep the deep oceans liquid for a very long time. {c17} The cold is only a thought experiment. {-}

## close {shot: you at dusk, the street warm, the thermometer reading 20 °C · energy: 0.4} (__)
vo: So the next time you feel cold, remember how little of the scale you have used. {-} From the warmth of a room, to the coldest day on Earth, to the edge of the universe's coldest places, you have only travelled a few hundred degrees. {c1} And the floor is still not there. {-} Thanks for watching. {-}
