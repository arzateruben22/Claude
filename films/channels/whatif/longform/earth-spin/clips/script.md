# Earth-spin pilot: five vertical cut-downs (script v1, for fact review; nothing rendered)

Five 9:16 clips (1080x1920/30, 30 to 60 s each), one per milestone of "What If Earth Spun Faster and Couldn't Stop?" (script.md in the folder above). Rendered natively in
9:16 from the same scenes with the camera reframed for vertical (eye line and over the shoulder, not a crop of the 16:9). Each clip stands alone: a 2 s hook of its own,
one milestone, a cliffhanger into the next. Voice lines are reused verbatim from the long script (marked `R:` with the beat they come from; their claims carry over); only
the hook and the outro lines are new (`N:`). Series line, "Thought experiment" badge, AI voice label on each. The same live readouts as the long video: the big "length of a
day" number and your speed under it, never resetting within a clip. House rules apply (one text element per idea; gaps up to 1.2 s; motion every 2 to 3 s).

End card on every clip: "Full video on YouTube: What If Earth Spun Faster and Couldn't Stop?" and "Part N of 5", with the series line.

## clip 1: twelve hours (target 40 s)
N: Earth's day is now twelve hours long. Here's what you'd feel. {-}
R(twelve): At a twelve hour day, sunrise to sunset takes six hours. {c4} The ground under your feet now moves at about thirty-three hundred kilometers an hour, nearly three times the speed of sound. {c2} {c14} You weigh one point four percent less than on a still Earth, four times what you feel today. {c5} A seventy kilogram person feels like about sixty-nine. {c5} {c13}
N: Doesn't sound like much. But the air is about to turn on you. {-}

## clip 2: the air can't keep up (target 40 s)
N: The ground is moving faster than sound, and the air hasn't noticed. {-}
R(wind): The ground speeds up at once; the atmosphere is pulled along only by friction, and that takes time. {-} For the first hours, the air is left behind. {c7} At a twelve hour day, the ground is moving through the air at about four hundred and sixty meters a second, faster than the speed of sound. {c7} {c14} Winds like that would strip the surface, and they would only be the beginning. {-}
N: And then the oceans start to move. {-}

## clip 3: the oceans leave the poles (target 50 s)
N: Speed the Earth up fast, and the oceans leave the poles. {-}
R(oceans): A spinning planet pushes water outward, toward the equator, exactly as it pushes you. {-} Speed the spin up quickly and the ground cannot adjust in time. At a twelve hour day, the water wants to be about thirty-three kilometers higher at the equator than at the poles. {c6} The whole ocean is only about three point seven kilometers deep. {c18} So the water does not simply rise. It drains toward a belt around the equator, while the poles dry out. {c6} This is a rough, first guess, but the direction is certain. {-}
N: That was a twelve hour day. Wait until it's two. {-}

## clip 4: half your weight (target 40 s)
N: A two hour day, and you weigh half of what you did. {-}
R(two): At two hours, you weigh half of what you did. {c5} Twenty thousand kilometers an hour; the Sun crosses the whole sky in an hour. {c2} {c4} Every object near the equator is half as heavy as it should be. {-} Buildings, bridges and oceans are all being asked to hold on with half the force. {-}
N: And in half an hour, you stop touching the ground. {-}

## clip 5: you float (target 55 s)
N: At an hour and twenty-four minutes, you're in orbit, standing still. {-}
R(float): At a day of about an hour and twenty-four minutes, the ground under you moves at seven point nine kilometers a second. {c8} That is the speed of a satellite in a low orbit. {c8} At that speed, the push outward exactly cancels gravity. {c8} You weigh nothing. {-} You are no longer standing on the Earth. You are orbiting it, one stride above the surface, with the whole equator going by beneath you. {-}
R(break): And past that point, the equator can no longer keep its own rock. {c10} This is as far as a simple sum can honestly take us. {-}
N: Watch how it ends, in the full video. {-}
