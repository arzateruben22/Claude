# Possibility Lab · Long form pilot: "What If Earth Spun Faster and Couldn't Stop?" (script v1; fact-check before any render)

About 9 min, 16:9 (2560x1440), for YouTube's main feed, narrated by a synthetic voice (labelled as AI), with an original score. A thought experiment:
the Earth's spin speeds up every second, nothing can slow it, and you stand on the equator the whole time. Every number comes from `calc.py`
(arithmetic on NASA's Earth fact sheet values); `claims.json` ties each sentence to a source or marks it a derivation. All claims are OPEN: none has been
opened by a person yet.

The picture: first person, from your eye line and over your shoulder, on the graph-paper equator under a sky that visibly speeds up. ONE big live number is always
on screen, "length of a day", falling from 24 h to 1.4 h and never resetting; your speed at the equator is the smaller number under it. Each milestone is a beat
where something visibly changes. House rules: one text element per idea; the caption carries the sentence; at most two text blocks besides the series line, badges
and disclaimers; no voiceless gap over 1.2 s; something moves every 2 to 3 s; badge "Thought experiment" throughout.

One line per beat. `*starred*` words get a camera landing or a sound hit. `{c1,c2}` after a sentence ties it to `claims.json`; `{-}` marks a sentence that states
nothing outside the film.

## hook {shot: you at the equator at dawn, over your shoulder; the Sun is a slow disk; the day counter reads 24 h; the sky begins to quicken · badge: thought experiment · title: "What If Earth Spun Faster and Couldn't Stop?" · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: What if the Earth started to spin faster, a little more every second, and nothing in the universe could stop it? {-} You are standing on the equator, and you are going to watch what happens, minute by minute, until the planet can't take it anymore. {-}

## premise {shot: the readout appears: "length of a day" 24 h and "your speed" 1,674 km/h; a thin timeline shows 24 h falling to 1.4 h · milestone: the rules of the thought experiment · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: Here are the rules. {-} The Earth keeps its size, its shape, its air and its water. {-} Only the spin changes, and it only ever gets faster. {-} On screen there is one number that matters: the length of a day. {-} It starts at twenty-four hours, and by the end it will have fallen to about an hour and twenty-four minutes. {c8} Under it is the second number: how fast the ground is moving under your feet. {-} Nothing resets, and nothing slows. {-} The planet is not a perfect ball, and we will skip a great deal of real physics, so treat every number as a first guess. {c13}

## today {shot: your eye line; the Sun crosses the sky in twelve hours; a speed readout shows how fast the ground already moves under you · energy: 0.45} (__)
vo: Right now, one day lasts twenty-four hours. {c1} Or almost: the Earth turns once in twenty-three hours, fifty-six minutes. {c1} Standing on the equator, the ground carries you around at about sixteen hundred and seventy kilometers an hour. {c2} You never feel it, because everything moves with you. {-} What you do feel is a tiny push outward. You weigh about a third of a percent less than you would on a planet that did not spin. {c3} {-}

## spinup {shot: the day counter falls: 24, 20, 16 h; the Sun starts to climb noticeably faster; shadows swing · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: Now the spin begins to speed up. {-} At twenty hours, you can see the shadows sweep. {-} At sixteen, the Sun climbs the sky like a slow elevator. {-} The rule never changes. A day only ever gets shorter, and you cannot turn it back. {-}

## twelve {shot: the counter passes 12 h; the Sun crosses the sky in six hours; speed readout 3,340 km/h · milestone: a twelve hour day · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: At a twelve hour day, sunrise to sunset takes six hours. {c4} The ground under your feet now moves at about thirty-three hundred kilometers an hour, nearly three times the speed of sound. {c2} {c14} You weigh one point four percent less than on a still Earth, four times what you feel today. {c5} A seventy kilogram person feels like about sixty-nine. {c5} {c13}

## wind {shot: the air above you: dust and clouds streaming the wrong way; wind speed readout · milestone: the atmosphere cannot keep up · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: But the air has a problem. {-} The ground speeds up at once; the atmosphere is pulled along only by friction, and that takes time. {-} For the first hours, the air is left behind. {c7} At a twelve hour day, the ground is moving through the air at about four hundred and sixty meters a second, faster than the speed of sound. {c7} {c14} Winds like that would strip the surface, and they would only be the beginning. {-}

## oceans {shot: wide view from your shoulder: the sea surface tilts; water slides toward the equator; at the poles the seabed shows · milestone: the oceans move · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: And then the oceans begin to move. {-} A spinning planet pushes water outward, toward the equator, exactly as it pushes you. {-} Today the Earth already bulges about twenty-one kilometers wider at the equator than from pole to pole, and the ground has had billions of years to settle into that shape. {c12} Speed the spin up quickly and the ground cannot adjust in time. At a twelve hour day, the water wants to stand about thirty-three kilometers higher at the equator than at the poles, extra, compared with today. {c6} The whole ocean is only about three point seven kilometers deep. {c18} So the water does not simply rise. It drains toward a belt around the equator, while the poles dry out. {c6} This is a rough, first guess, but the direction is certain: land near the poles emerges, and your own coast is flooded. {-}

## weather {shot: from your shoulder: a storm system swings round a circling pattern; a Coriolis readout compares today, six hours, 1.4 hours · milestone: the weather tightens · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: The sky has another problem. {-} Storms are steered by the planet's spin, through the Coriolis effect, and that steering grows in step with the spin rate. {c15} At a six hour day it is four times stronger than today. {c15} At the edge of floating, it is seventeen times stronger. {c15} Weather systems would be squeezed into tight, fast bands, and the calm of an ordinary afternoon would be gone. {-} Nobody could forecast it, because nobody has seen a planet like this. {-}

## six {shot: the counter passes 6 h; the Sun races; sunrise to sunset takes three hours · milestone: a six hour day · energy: 0.65} (__)
vo: At a six hour day, the Sun races from horizon to horizon in three hours. {c4} The equator moves at nearly seven thousand kilometers an hour. {c2} {c4} You are five and a half percent lighter. {c5} Jumping now takes you noticeably higher than it should, and every step feels slightly wrong. {-}

## calendar {shot: a wall calendar over your shoulder: the days flick by far faster than the pages; a year counter reads days in a year · milestone: the calendar breaks · energy: 0.5} (__)
vo: Meanwhile, the calendar breaks. {-} The year does not change, because the Earth still goes round the Sun once. {-} But every day gets shorter, so a year contains more days. {c16} Right now, at a six hour day, a year already holds about fourteen hundred and sixty days. {c20} By the end, it would hold over six thousand, {c16} with a sunrise every eighty-four minutes, and you would be asked to sleep eight times a day. {-}

## poles {shot: the camera lifts and moves north over the globe; a second figure at the pole stands still; the readouts compare the two places · milestone: the poles are different · energy: 0.55} (__)
vo: Not everyone on Earth would feel the same thing. {-} At the poles, the ground only turns on the spot. Nobody there is carried around at all, and they would weigh the same as ever. {-} At forty-five degrees north, you move at seventy percent of the equator's speed, and the push outward is only half as strong. {c17} {c5} The farther you stand from the equator, the longer you keep your weight. {-} But the water leaves the poles first, so the stillest place on Earth would stand high and dry. {c6}

## three {shot: the counter passes 3 h; the Sun crosses the sky in ninety minutes; the horizon begins to curve visibly; weight readout · milestone: a three hour day · energy: 0.7} (__)
vo: At a three hour day, the Sun crosses the sky in just ninety minutes. {c4} The ground now moves at over thirteen thousand kilometers an hour. {c2} {c4} You weigh twenty-two percent less. {c5} Seventy kilograms feels like fifty-five. {c5} {c13}

## two {shot: the counter passes 2 h; the Sun is a streak; you are lifting off your heels · milestone: half your weight · energy: 0.75} (__)
vo: At two hours, you weigh half of what you did. {c5} Twenty thousand kilometers an hour; the Sun crosses the whole sky in an hour. {c2} {c4} Every object near the equator is half as heavy as it should be. {-} Buildings, bridges and oceans are all being asked to hold on with half the force. {-}

## one-five {shot: the counter reads 1.5 h; you stand on tiptoe; the rim of the Earth is a blur beyond · milestone: almost weightless · energy: 0.8} (__)
vo: At an hour and a half, you are almost weightless. {c5} You weigh about one eighth of what you did. {c5} The equator moves at twenty-seven thousand kilometers an hour. {c2} One more nudge, and something changes completely. {-}

## float {shot: the counter reads 1.4 h; the ground slides away under your feet; the speed readout reads 7.9 km/s · milestone: orbital speed at the equator · energy: 0.9} (__)
vo: At a day of about an hour and twenty-four minutes, the ground under you moves at seven point nine kilometers a second. {c8} That is the speed of a satellite in a low orbit. {c8} At that speed, the push outward exactly cancels gravity. {c8} You weigh nothing. {-} You are no longer standing on the Earth. You are orbiting it, one stride above the surface, with the whole equator going by beneath you. {-}

## break {shot: the counter reads 1.4 h and holds as the equator's rim lifts: dust and rock rise · milestone: the end of the thought experiment · energy: 0.85} (__)
vo: And past that point, the equator can no longer keep its own rock. {c10} This is as far as a simple sum can honestly take us. {-} A real planet is not a perfect ball, and what happens next depends on how strong the Earth is. {c10} But the direction is clear: the spin would tear the equator apart. {c10} First the loose things would go, then the sea, then the rock. {-}

## cost {shot: the whole Earth, spinning, with the energy bar: spin energy now versus then · milestone: where would the energy come from · energy: 0.6} (__)
vo: Of course, nothing in the real universe could ever do this. {-} To spin the Earth that fast you would have to put in about six times ten to the thirty-first joules, nearly three hundred times the spin energy it has today. {c9} That is every drop of sunlight the Earth receives, for about eleven million years. {c9} {c11}

vo: So the next time you stand still, remember the speed you are carrying. {-} You are already moving at sixteen hundred kilometers an hour. {c2} You only have to go about seventeen times faster to leave the ground behind. {c2} {c8} The day would have to shrink from twenty-four hours to an hour and twenty-four minutes. {c8} That is the whole distance between standing and flying, and it is why a planet's spin can never be a small thing. {-} Thanks for watching. {-}
