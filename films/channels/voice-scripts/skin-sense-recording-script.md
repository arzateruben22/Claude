# Skin Sense: host recording script (Season 2)

This is the narration for all six Season 2 episodes. The words are exactly what's on screen and what has been
fact-checked, so please read them as written. If something doesn't feel natural to say, mark the line and we'll
reword it and re-check the fact.

### The voice we want
**Bright, happy and genuinely excited**, like you just found out something amazing your skin does and can't wait to tell a friend.
Warm and smiling, but still clear and trustworthy, because it's science.
- **Smile while you read** (it really changes the sound).
- **Bold words** are the "wow" of the line: give them a lift.
- Questions should sound curious and playful.
- Don't rush: clear and lively beats fast.
- The channel never gives advice, so keep it wonder, not instruction.

### How to record
- **Room:** quiet, with soft stuff around you (a closet full of clothes or a bedroom; avoid bathrooms and kitchens).
- **Phone:** Voice Memos is fine. Hold it **a hand's width (15–20 cm) from your mouth**, slightly to the side. Same spot every time.
- **One file per episode.** Read the lines in order with a **2-second pause** between them. Flubbed one? Pause, then read **that whole line again**; we keep the last take.
- Start each file by saying its name, e.g. *"Skin Sense, Season 2, Episode 1"*.
- **Name the files** `ss-s2e01.m4a` … `ss-s2e06.m4a`, and `ss-cta.m4a` for the like and follow lines at the end.

## S2 Ep 1: “Why goosebumps happen”

Say it like: **follicle**: say *FOL-ih-kul*

**1.** Why do you get **goosebumps**? A tiny muscle on every hair pulls.

**2.** Cold makes nerves in your skin send a signal. That signal tells a tiny muscle beside each hair to tighten.

**3.** Under the skin, each tiny muscle is woven through with nerve fibres. At its other end, it is attached to the bulge of the hair follicle, the pocket the hair grows from.

**4.** When the nerve fires, the muscle shortens and pulls the hair upright. In people, that no longer helps keep us warm. Yet the muscle, the nerve and the follicle are still wired together across mammals.

**5.** And this wiring does more than raise hair. In mice, the nerve talks to the stem cells that regrow **hair**. The muscle keeps that nerve in place.

**6.** So goosebumps are a muscle, a nerve and a hair, working together. A small reflex, and in mice, a bigger **job**.

**7.** Next: why fingers wrinkle in water. Your **nerves** are in charge.

## S2 Ep 2: “Why fingers wrinkle in water”


**1.** Why do fingers **wrinkle**? It's your nerves, not only the water.

**2.** After about fifteen minutes in water, the hairless skin on your hands and feet has folded deeply. It is an active process, run by your nervous system.

**3.** When nerves tell the tiny blood vessels in your fingertips to narrow, the tissue below shrinks a little. Scientists think the skin above then folds into ridges.

**4.** Doctors have used this to test nerves for over eighty years. When the nerves to a finger stop working, the finger does not wrinkle. So the wrinkles are a sign of working nerves, not just soaking.

**5.** Why would the body do this? One idea is grip. In one study, people handled wet objects faster with wrinkled fingers. A later study found no **difference**.

**6.** So wrinkled fingers are your nerves at work. Whether they help you grip is still **debated**.

**7.** Next: how sweat really cools you. Cooling happens when sweat **dries**.

## S2 Ep 3: “How sweat cools you”


**1.** How does sweat **cool** you? It works by drying.

**2.** You have about two to four million sweat glands. They sit in both hairy and hairless skin. Each is a tiny coil with a thin tube that leads to the surface. When you heat up, they release sweat.

**3.** As the water in sweat turns to vapour, it takes heat from your skin. Each gram of sweat that dries carries away about two thousand four hundred joules.

**4.** That is why you sweat more in hot places and during hard exercise. When heavy clothing covers the skin, less heat escapes by evaporation. Your body is using water to move heat out.

**5.** Here is the twist. Sweat's job is cooling, not **cleaning**. Waste leaves mostly through the kidneys and gut.

**6.** So sweat cools you when it dries. Its job is cooling, not **cleaning**.

**7.** Next: what a tan really is. Pigment can form a cap over a cell's nucleus.

## S2 Ep 4: “What a tan really is”

Say it like: **melanin**: say *MEL-uh-nin* · **UVB**: say *U-V-B* · **UVA**: say *U-V-A*

**1.** What is a tan, **really**? Pigment, parked over each cell's nucleus.

**2.** Brown melanin is made in special pigment cells. Their arms hand it to the skin cells around them. After ultraviolet light, it can gather over each cell's nucleus, like a small cap.

**3.** That cap works like a tiny parasol, absorbing and scattering ultraviolet light. In skin samples exposed to UVB, cells with a cap had less DNA damage.

**4.** The more melanin a cell held, the more protection it had. So the shield is real, but its strength varies from cell to cell. In lab-grown skin cells, the long rays, UVA, set off cap-building through a light sensor.

**5.** Here is the twist. Caps lower the damage, but do not **erase** it. The effect depends on how much melanin a cell holds.

**6.** So a tan is pigment gathered over each cell's nucleus. It lowers damage, but does not **erase** it.

**7.** Next: the brick wall on your skin. Which part holds water **in**?

## S2 Ep 5: “The brick wall on your skin”

Say it like: **stratum corneum**: say *STRAY-tum KOR-nee-um*

**1.** Why don't you dry **out**? Your skin has a brick wall.

**2.** Its outer layer, the stratum corneum, is the barrier. Flat, tough cells are the bricks. Fats between them are the mortar. A scientist named Peter Elias called it that.

**3.** The mortar is stacked sheets of fat, wrapped around each cell. That makes a semi-permeable barrier, which is what lets life live on land.

**4.** That wall is the skin's first line of defence. It keeps outside things out and holds water in. It is not a single tough sheet. It is a system of cells, fats and acids.

**5.** Here is the twist. The bricks are not what holds water in. The fats between them do that **work**. Water cannot easily cross that mortar.

**6.** So your skin's wall is bricks and fats, working as a team. The fats are the seal.

**7.** Next: fingerprints, formed before birth. The pattern spreads in **waves**.

## S2 Ep 6: “Fingerprints form before you're born”

Say it like: **follicle**: say *FOL-ih-kul*

**1.** When do your fingerprints **form**? Before you are born.

**2.** In the womb, the skin on each fingertip starts building ridges. Each ridge is a band of skin cells that keeps growing. Each ridge begins like a hair follicle, then stops partway.

**3.** Chemical signals push and pull against each other, in what scientists call a Turing system. It turns smooth skin into bands of growth.

**4.** Ridges do not appear all at once. They start at a few places on the fingertip and spread outward in waves. Think of ripples spreading on sand. Where the waves meet shapes the type of pattern.

**5.** Here is the twist. The pattern is grown, not drawn. Each place a wave starts is a little different. Tiny differences in where waves begin make endless **variety**.

**6.** So a fingerprint is a pattern of waves, set before birth. Yours is one of endless **variations**.
