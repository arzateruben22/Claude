# One More Orbit Season 3: your recording script

Season 3 is one long video, “The Moon Starts Coming Closer. It Won't Stop.”, cut into 8 short parts. This is everything you read for it.
The words are exactly what's on screen and what has been fact-checked, so read them as written. If a phrase doesn't
feel natural in your mouth, say which line and it gets reworded and re-checked (the captions are rebuilt from your
recording, so they'll match what you say).

### How to record (5 minutes of setup, then it's easy)
- **Room:** quiet, with soft stuff around you (a closet of clothes or a bedroom is great; a bathroom or kitchen echoes).
- **Phone:** Voice Memos is fine. Hold it **about a hand's width (15–20 cm) from your mouth**, slightly to the side. Same spot every session.
- **Pauses:** 2 seconds between lines. Flubbed one? Pause, then read **that whole line again**; the last take is kept.
- **Start each file by saying its name**, e.g. *“One More Orbit, Season 3, long video”*.
- **Files:** `oo-s3.m4a` (Part A), `oo-s3-parts.m4a` (Part B), `part-closers.m4a` (Part C, once ever) and `oo-cta.m4a` (Part D, once per channel).
- **Upload:** https://github.com/arzateruben22/claude/upload/claude/space-s2/films/channels/space/voice-owner

### How it should sound
Quiet awe. Slower and softer than Possibility Lab, as if you're standing outside at night telling one friend what you can see. Let the big numbers land, then breathe.
- **Bold words** are the punch of the line: lean on them a bit. Say numbers slowly; they're the payoff.
- Cliffhangers (the last line of each chapter) hang: a little slower, with a lift at the end.

## Part A. The long video: “The Moon Starts Coming Closer. It Won't Stop.”
File: `oo-s3.m4a`. Read straight through; take a breath between lines.

**1.** You're on a beach at night.

**2.** The sea is calm, and the Moon hangs over the water. Right now, it is drifting away from you. About three point eight centimeters a year. Tonight, imagine it reverses. It starts coming closer, and it never stops. Nothing here is a forecast. It is a thought experiment, built from real physics. First, where it really is. On average, the Moon is about three hundred eighty-four thousand kilometers away. From the beach, it looks about half a degree wide. About the same size as the Sun. Its light takes about a second and a quarter to reach you. Everything in this film starts from that ordinary view. Same sea, same sand, same Moon. Only one thing will change. Keep an eye on the number in the corner. It only ever goes down.

**3.** And the first thing that changes is not what you'd expect.

**4.** One quarter of the way in. Three quarters of today's distance: about two hundred eighty-eight thousand kilometers. The Moon looks one and a third times wider. You'd notice it right away, like a coin held a little closer. Its light takes about a second to reach you now. Picture a Moon just a little too big for the sky. The craters you know are easier to pick out. You almost doubt yourself. Did it grow, or did you imagine it? The rest of the night looks almost the same. The stars are the same. The sea is calm. Even so, there is one thing the sea already feels. The Moon's pull on the Earth gets stronger as it comes in. At this distance, its tidal pull is more than twice what it is today. Not enough to see on the beach, not yet. But the number keeps falling, and the sea is about to start to move.

**5.** Half the distance is where the water starts to climb.

**6.** Half the distance: about one hundred ninety-two thousand kilometers. The Moon now looks twice as wide as it does tonight. Tides come from the difference in the Moon's pull across the Earth. That difference falls off with the cube of the distance. So half the distance means about eight times the tidal force. Here is the idea, not a measured height: the water at your feet starts to climb the beach. Slowly, in long swells, hours at a time. The waterline reaches the dry sand, and keeps going. Then it falls back, and comes up again higher. Breathe, and watch the sea. It is not a wave, and it is not a storm. It is a slow, patient rise, as if the whole ocean were being lifted from above. The Moon is no longer a calm light in the sky. It is something that pulls.

**7.** And the night itself is about to change.

**8.** One hundred fifty thousand kilometers. The Moon looks about two and a half times wider. Look around you. The beach is brighter. Moonlight falls off with the square of the distance, for the same phase. So at this distance, a full Moon gives about six and a half times the light. Your shadow on the sand is sharp. The faint stars near the Moon are washed out. And the Moon is crossing the sky faster. Closer orbits are faster orbits. At this distance, one lap takes about seven days, not twenty-seven. Imagine the sand, pale as paper. Your own shadow is long and sharp. The water has a silver road across it, wider than any you have seen. Night is not as dark as it used to be, and it is about to get a different kind of strange. By now, the Moon looks far bigger than the Sun.

**9.** Which means the next time they line up, something happens in the daytime.

**10.** One hundred thousand kilometers. The Moon is nearly four times wider than tonight. The Sun and the Moon look about the same size from Earth today. That is why a total solar eclipse is possible at all. Today, a total solar eclipse happens somewhere on Earth about every year and a half. Here, the Moon is much bigger than the Sun. So when they line up, its shadow on Earth is wider. And eclipses would be far more common. Not every month, though, because the Moon's orbit is tilted by a few degrees. But often. The sky goes dim, and the stars come out in the daytime. A long dark noon, with the Sun's edge glowing around a huge black disc. Then the light returns. And then you look at the Moon again, and it has moved.

**11.** Closer. Faster. And the ground under you is feeling it too.

**12.** Seventy thousand kilometers. The Moon is about five and a half times wider than tonight. Its tidal pull is more than a hundred sixty times today's. The sea is not the only thing that moves. The solid Earth already rises and falls a little with the Moon, tens of centimeters a day. With far more pull, it would flex far more. What that would do to faults and volcanoes, this film cannot tell you. Researchers study it, and it is not settled. We would rather say that than guess. Imagine the sand under your feet, and the sea that rises and holds. Boats at anchor ride very high. A harbor does not look like a harbor any more. What we can say is simpler. In the picture, the sea stays high now: we show the idea, not a measured height. And the number keeps falling.

**13.** Because soon, the Moon will rise from the wrong side of the sky.

**14.** Fifty thousand kilometers. The Moon is nearly eight times wider than tonight. It fills a big part of the sky. The pull is about four hundred fifty times today's. One lap now takes about a day and a quarter. That is close to how fast the Earth turns. A moon that orbits slower than the Earth turns rises in the east, like today. A moon that orbits faster than the Earth turns rises in the west. The line between them is about forty-two thousand kilometers. In this picture its orbit is right over the equator. Watch the number. Forty-two thousand. The Moon hangs in your sky, almost still. Then it begins to slide the wrong way across it. The Sun still rises in the east. The Moon rises in the west. But the number keeps falling.

**15.** And there is a line ahead that nothing can hold together past.

**16.** Thirty-five thousand kilometers. Each step takes a little less time than the last. Thirty thousand. Twenty-five. Twenty. There is a distance where tides pull harder on a body than its own gravity holds it together. It is called the Roche limit. For a Moon like ours around Earth, it is around eighteen thousand kilometers, if the Moon behaves like a fluid. Around nine and a half thousand, if it is perfectly rigid. A real Moon sits somewhere in between. At eighteen thousand kilometers, it looks more than twenty times wider than tonight. It is at least as wide as a fist held out at arm's length, and wider still overhead. It is not a Moon you look at any more. It is a wall of grey, and its surface is straining. The number is nearly there. Eighteen thousand. Seventeen.

**17.** And then the Moon does something no Moon should survive.

**18.** And then it comes apart. Not an explosion. A stretch. The side facing you is pulled harder than the side facing away, until the Moon cannot hold together. Rock and dust spread along its old orbit. Imagine the pieces, each one a small Moon, drifting apart. Over time, material like that can flatten into a ring around a planet. A whole Moon's worth of rock, spread out like a veil. Ready for what that looks like from your beach? Tonight you look up. There is no Moon. A thin, bright band crosses the sky from horizon to horizon. That is how a ring looks from the ground. The real Moon is still drifting away, three point eight centimeters a year. Tonight's Moon is fine. Thanks for watching this thought experiment.

**19.** You're on a beach at night.
