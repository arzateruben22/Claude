## This episode
1. **Claims.** A physicist works through `CLAIMS-REVIEW.md`: every claim was checked from search excerpts only, so a person opens each link and ticks it.
2. **The heights are computed, the climb's pace isn't.** h = Δp/ρg with ρ = 998.2 kg/m³ and g = 9.81 m/s²: the sip lifts the drink 19.3 cm (19 hPa); the lungs' best (about 100 cm of water) lifts the tall straw's water 1.00 m; a perfect pump leaves water's own vapor (23.4 hPa at 20 °C), so the column stops 10.11 m above the basin, and the measure's 10.3 m is the column that weighs as much as the air above it (1.03 kg per cm²), explained on a card ("Why it stops at 10.1 m: water's own vapor fills the top"). Heights on the house's scale count from the basin's surface. How fast the tall column climbs is an illustration, badged "Climb speed: illustration".
3. **The lid (the change).** The glass is filled completely and an airtight lid is sealed round the straw: no air presses on the drink and water barely compresses, so a hard sip (the spine reads 915 hPa in the straw, about the lungs' typical best) lifts nothing. This is the jar-lid demonstration in c2's source ("You need the lid off in order for this experiment to work"). The first draft pumped the air out of a jar around an open straw; the review showed room air would then bubble down the straw, so it was replaced. On screen: "Rigid lid, no air inside".
4. **"Your lungs alone? About one meter."** Typical maximal inspiratory pressure is 70–125 cm of water (men about 100, women about 70), so "about one meter" is the typical adult's best from the breathing muscles; many people manage less.
5. **"Pumping with cheeks and tongue, one stunt reached seven."** (c15) Veritasium's "World's Longest (Vertical) Straw" (2012) got water about 7 m up by mouth. The source is a STEM Learning listing of the video, checked from its search excerpt ("The best result was about 7 metres"): watch the video to confirm the height and that it was done by mouth, pumping with cheeks and tongue. The coordinating session's audit also cited a mouth-suction study we could not find in our own searches, so it is not cited. On screen the column reaches 7.00 m on "seven." and a tick marks it ("one stunt: about 7 m").
