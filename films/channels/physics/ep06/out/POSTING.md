**Title**

The ten-meter straw | Rest Frame No. 006

**Description**

```
What lifts a drink up a straw? Something invisible, pushing.

In 1644, Torricelli saw why. We live at the bottom of an ocean of air, and it presses on everything. A sip lowers the pressure inside the straw, and the air outside pushes the drink up. How hard can it push? Straws don't pull. The atmosphere pushes, and its push runs out near ten meters.

Series: Season 1: Forces you can see, part 6 of 6.

Sources
1. Standard sea-level air pressure is 1013.25 hPa, and that pressure is (approximately) the weight of the air above: about 10.1 N, the weight of about 1.03 kg, on each square centimetre. — noaa.gov: https://www.noaa.gov/jetstream/atmosphere/air-pressure
2. With a glass filled to the brim and a rigid lid sealed around the straw, no air presses on the drink and water barely compresses, so sipping lowers the pressure in the straw but lifts nothing: a straw needs air pressing on the drink. — semissourian.com: https://www.semissourian.com/features/straw-science-1464828
3. In 1644 Evangelista Torricelli explained the limit of suction pumps by the weight of the air, with his mercury column held up by atmospheric pressure and a vacuum above it. — aps.org: https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2012/10/torricelli-demonstrates-existence-vacuum
4. Torricelli wrote to Michelangelo Ricci on 11 June 1644: 'Noi viviamo sommersi nel fondo d'un pelago d'aria' ('we live submerged at the bottom of an ocean of air'); the air's weight presses on every surface. — Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelista_Torricelli
5. Sipping lowers the pressure inside the straw; the atmosphere pressing on the drink pushes it up the straw. — ipm.org: https://www.ipm.org/show/amomentofscience/2023-11-21/air-pressure-and-drinking-straws
6. Water boils when the pressure on it falls to its vapour pressure, about 2.34 kPa at 20 °C; above the column only that vapour remains, so the water at the top boils unheated. — people.bu.edu: https://people.bu.edu/straub/courses/demomaster/h2oroomboil.html
7. Healthy adults' strongest suction from the breathing muscles alone (maximal inspiratory pressure) is typically about 70–125 cm of water, so lungs alone could hold water in a straw only about 0.7–1.25 m up. — NIH: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9817693/
8. A column of water 1 cm² across and 10.3 m tall weighs about 1.03 kg, the same as the air above each square centimetre at sea level. — e-education.psu.edu: https://www.e-education.psu.edu/meteo3/print/book/export/html/2000
9. Ice floats on liquid water: freezing makes water about 9% less dense. — usgs.gov: https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-density
10. Sucking with the mouth (cheeks and tongue working as a pump), the Veritasium team lifted water about 7 m up a tube in 2012 ('World's Longest Straw'); lungs alone can't get near that. — www-legacy.stem.org.uk: https://www-legacy.stem.org.uk/resources/elibrary/resource/424985/worlds-longest-vertical-straw
```
