Thrown or dropped? | Rest Frame No. 001

**Description**

```
Which ball lands first? One thrown sideways, one just dropped.

Old gunnery books drew cannonballs flying straight, then dropping. Galileo saw two motions at once: a steady glide across, plus a fall. Gravity pulls only down, so at every tick, both sit at the same height. Speed decides where a ball lands. Height alone decides when. That holds even for a bullet fired level.

Series: Season 1: Forces you can see, part 1 of 6. Next: the swing's secret

Sources
1. Without air resistance, a falling object accelerates steadily at g ≈ 9.81 m/s², so it covers more distance in each equal interval. — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/3-5-free-fall
2. A level throw at 0.44 m/s from 1 m lands 19.9 cm out, after the same 0.4515 s as the drop, because horizontal and vertical motion are independent when air is negligible. — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/4-3-projectile-motion
3. In Two New Sciences (1638), Fourth Day, Galileo treats a projectile's motion as compounded of a uniform horizontal motion and a naturally accelerated vertical one. — UVA Galileo and Einstein: https://galileoandeinstein.phys.virginia.edu/tns244.htm
4. One pendulum's period is nearly independent of its amplitude, especially below about 15°; at 30° it is about 1.7% longer.
```
