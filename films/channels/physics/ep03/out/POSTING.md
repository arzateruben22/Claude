**Title**

Where does the bounce go? | Rest Frame No. 003

**Description**

```
Why can't it reach your hand? Somewhere, nearly half its bounce disappeared.

We call that energy lost. James Joule wouldn't. In the eighteen-forties, he let falling weights turn a paddle in water, and the water warmed. Energy doesn't vanish. It becomes heat, shaking and sound, spread too thin to see. A bounce never destroys energy. It gives it away, knock by knock.

Series: Season 1: Forces you can see, part 3 of 6.

Sources
1. A tennis ball dropped onto a hard floor comes back to a little over half its drop height: the ITF approves balls that, dropped from 254 cm onto concrete, rebound to 135–147 cm (53–58%). The bench's ball keeps 56% (restitution 0.75): 100 cm down, 56 cm up. — twu.tennis-warehouse.com: https://twu.tennis-warehouse.com/learning_center/balltesting.php
2. Part of a bounce's kinetic energy leaves as sound (a collision that loses kinetic energy to heat, sound or vibration is inelastic). — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/college-physics/pages/8-5-inelastic-collisions-in-one-dimension
3. A tennis ball regains its round shape after the squash but pushes back with less force than it took to squash it (hysteresis in the rubber), so it returns less energy than went in. — lida.sport-iat.de: https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4005426?lng=en
4. In the 1840s James Joule drove paddle wheels in a vessel of water with falling weights, and measured the water warming with a thermometer; the weights' lost energy became heat (British Association abstract 1845; Philosophical Transactions 1850). — jimclifford.ca: https://jimclifford.ca/philosophical-transactions-ocr-1665-1869/articles/jstor-108427/
5. Energy is conserved: the kinetic energy that leaves a bounce is converted into other forms (thermal energy, sound, vibration), not destroyed. — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/8-3-conservation-of-energy
6. Dropped stacked, the heavy basketball bounces off the floor first and then hits the light tennis ball coming down: it hands most of its energy to the tennis ball and leaves much slower (with perfect bounces, at 0.65 of its speed, 43% of its height). — demos.smu.ca: https://demos.smu.ca/demos/mechanics/143-double-ball-drop
7. With perfect bounces the tennis ball leaves at 2.65 times its landing speed and rises 7.0 times as high as it fell; real balls lose part of that but still fly well above the height they were dropped from. (The ideal limit for a very heavy lower ball is 3× the speed and 9× the height.) — sites.physics.unt.edu: https://sites.physics.unt.edu/~klittler/demo_room/mech_demos/1N30_60.html
8. A 57 g tennis ball that falls 100 cm and comes back to 56 cm is missing 44 cm of height: m·g·Δh = 0.057 × 9.81 × 0.4375 = 0.245 J, a quarter of a joule. — m.itftennis.com: https://m.itftennis.com/media/2100/balls-ball-approval-tests.pdf
9. If all of the bounce's quarter joule stayed in the 57 g ball as heat, the ball would warm on average by about three thousandths of a degree Celsius. — m.itftennis.com: https://m.itftennis.com/media/2167/balls-ball-manufacture.pdf
10. In 1971 Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the Moon, and they landed together. — NASA: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_15_feather_drop.html
11. Joule held that what we now call energy is never destroyed: in 1843 he wrote that 'the grand agents of nature are, by the Creator's fiat, indestructible', and that wherever mechanical force is expended an exact equivalent of heat is obtained. — Nature: https://www.nature.com/articles/120280b0
```
