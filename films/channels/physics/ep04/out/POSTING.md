**Description**

```
Which lands first? A geology hammer, or a falcon feather?

For nearly 2,000 years, most scholars followed Aristotle: heavier things fall faster. Gravity does pull the hammer harder. But a heavier thing is harder to speed up, by exactly as much. So without air, they tie. Gravity speeds them up equally. Only the air tells them apart.

Series: Season 1: Forces you can see, part 4 of 6.

Sources
1. Dropped from about 1.6 m (Apollo 15's release height, a standing adult's shoulder) on Earth, a hammer falls in √(2 × 1.6 / 9.81) = 0.57 s; air changes a hammer's fall time negligibly over 1.6 m. — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/3-5-free-fall
2. When the hammer lands, the feather, slowed by air drag, is still in the air. — NASA: https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/drag-equation/
3. In 1971, during Apollo 15's last moonwalk, Commander David Scott dropped a 1.32 kg aluminium geological hammer and a falcon feather together from about 1.6 m, live on television. — NASA: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_15_feather_drop.html
4. After the hammer and feather landed together, Scott said: 'Mr Galileo was correct in his findings.' — physicsworld.com: https://physicsworld.com/a/the-legend-of-the-leaning-tower/
5. Aristotle held that heavier bodies fall faster than lighter ones, and his ideas on motion were accepted for nearly two thousand years (though challenged, e.g. by Philoponus in the 6th century). — mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Gravitation
6. By Newton's second law a = F/m: a mass twice as large needs twice the force for the same acceleration. With F = mg the masses cancel, a = g, provided gravitational mass equals inertial mass, which experiments confirm. — arxiv.org: https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15487v1
7. A 1.6 m fall at the Moon's surface gravity of 1.62 m/s² takes √(2 × 1.6 / 1.62) = 1.41 s. — NASA: https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/moonfact.html
8. On a freely spinning chair, pulling your arms (and anything in your hands) closer to the axis lowers your moment of inertia, so your spin rate rises to keep angular momentum constant. — OpenStax: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/11-3-conservation-of-angular-momentum
```
