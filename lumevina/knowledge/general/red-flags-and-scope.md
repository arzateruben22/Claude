# Red flags, contraindications and what an esthetician does

General esthetics knowledge, not Evelyn's words. This is why some questions always go to Evelyn, and some to a doctor. The chat uses it to explain the general rule in a sentence while handing a question to her; it never applies it to decide for a client.

## What an esthetician does, and doesn't

A licensed esthetician works on the surface of the skin to cleanse, exfoliate, improve and beautify: facials, superficial peels, dermaplaning, extractions, waxing, LED, skincare guidance and a home routine. An esthetician does not diagnose skin disease, treat medical conditions, prescribe or advise on medication, perform invasive or medical procedures, or promise results. When something looks medical, the answer is a referral to a doctor (a dermatologist for skin, the client's own doctor for medications and health). State boards set the exact limits; Evelyn knows hers.

## Always a doctor

- Trouble breathing, or swelling of the lips, tongue, throat or around the eyes: emergency, 911.
- A reaction that spreads, blisters, weeps, hives, or gets worse after the first day.
- Signs of infection: increasing pain, heat, pus, red streaks, fever.
- A mole or spot that is new, changing, asymmetrical, with an irregular border, more than one color, larger than a pencil eraser, or that bleeds or itches (the ABCDEs).
- Cystic, scarring or painful acne; acne that hasn't responded to months of consistent care; suspected hormonal acne.
- A named condition or a flare: eczema, psoriasis, rosacea, dermatitis, perioral dermatitis, fungal infections, cold sores, shingles, lupus and other autoimmune conditions.
- Anything about a prescription, a medication, a supplement interaction, pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Sudden hair loss, new dark patches, persistent itching, numbness.

## Standard contraindications (why a treatment waits)

These are the usual reasons an esthetician postpones or adapts a treatment. Each is a hand-off: the client's doctor and Evelyn decide, not the chat.

- **Accutane (isotretinoin):** skin is fragile and slow to heal during and after. Waxing, peels, dermaplaning and microchanneling generally wait six to twelve months after the last dose, with the doctor's OK. Gentle, hydrating facials are often fine.
- **Prescription retinoids** (tretinoin, adapalene, tazarotene) and strong acids: usually paused five to seven days before waxing, peels, dermaplaning and microchanneling, and for about a week after. Over-the-counter retinol and acids, three to five days.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** many estheticians avoid retinoids, high-strength salicylic acid, hydroquinone, some peels, and electrical or heat-based modalities, and switch to gentle, pregnancy-safe facials; sensitivity and melasma risk are higher. The client's doctor has the final word.
- **Recent Botox or fillers:** facials, massage and pressure on the area usually wait about two weeks; peels and dermaplaning, one to two weeks, per the injector.
- **Recent laser, peel, microneedling or surgery on the area:** wait until fully healed, often two to four weeks or longer.
- **Sunburn or a fresh tan:** no exfoliating treatments or waxing until it has settled.
- **Open skin, active breakouts with pustules, cold sores, rashes, infections:** treatments on that area wait.
- **Blood thinners, easy bruising, bleeding disorders:** extractions, microchanneling and firm massage need caution.
- **Keloid or hypertrophic scarring tendency:** microchanneling and peels need a doctor's OK.
- **Diabetes, autoimmune conditions, chemotherapy, radiation:** slower healing and higher infection risk; a doctor's clearance is standard.
- **Antibiotics (some, like doxycycline) and other photosensitizing medications:** more sun sensitivity, so peels and strong actives are timed carefully.
- **Metal implants, pacemakers, epilepsy:** some electrical modalities (high frequency, microcurrent) are skipped.
- **Under 18:** many estheticians need a parent present or signed consent, and keep treatments gentle.

## Allergies and sensitivities

Fragrance, essential oils, nuts (in some oils), latex (gloves, some wax strips), lanolin, certain preservatives, and sulfites. A client who has reacted before is patch-tested or given a different product. Any new rash is a reason to stop the product and message Evelyn.

## Results and guarantees

No esthetician can promise results. Skin responds to genetics, hormones, consistency and sun. The honest version: what usually happens, in what time frame, and what the client's part is.
