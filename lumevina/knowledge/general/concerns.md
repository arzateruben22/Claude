# Common concerns, in general

General esthetics knowledge, not Evelyn's words. What each concern usually is and what generally helps. Which products a particular client should use, and whether what they're seeing is a concern or a condition, is Evelyn's to say; and a named condition (eczema, rosacea, psoriasis, dermatitis, melasma that's new, anything infected) is a doctor's.

## Breakouts and acne

Acne is pores clogged by oil and dead skin, with bacteria and inflammation on top. **Non-inflamed** acne is blackheads (open, oxidized) and whiteheads (closed); **inflamed** acne is red papules, pustules, and deeper nodules and cysts. Most acne routines combine: gentle cleansing twice a day, an exfoliant that keeps pores clear (mandelic or salicylic acid), an antibacterial (benzoyl peroxide, sulfur), hydration that doesn't clog (gel moisturizers), SPF, and nothing in the routine that feeds it (heavy oils, comedogenic makeup, hair products on the hairline, dirty pillowcases, phones). It takes 8 to 12 weeks to judge, and consistency beats intensity. Cystic, scarring or painful acne, acne that hasn't moved after months of consistent care, or acne that's clearly hormonal usually needs a doctor alongside the esthetician.

## Purging vs. a breakout

**Purging** happens in the first two to six weeks after starting something that speeds turnover (acids, retinoids, benzoyl peroxide, an acne program): clogs that were already forming surface faster, in the places the client usually breaks out, as small whiteheads or bumps that clear quickly. It settles within about six weeks. **A breakout from a product** shows up in new places, with itching, redness or stinging, lasts longer, and keeps going. Purging is a reason to keep going (while easing frequency if it's harsh); a reaction is a reason to stop and message Evelyn.

## Dark spots and post-acne marks

Flat brown or red marks left after a breakout or irritation (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation or erythema). They fade on their own over months; faster with daily SPF, gentle exfoliation, brighteners (niacinamide, vitamin C, azelaic, tranexamic, arbutin, mandelic acid), and no picking. Deeper tones mark more easily and keep marks longer. Pitted or raised scars are texture, not pigment, and need collagen-building treatments.

## Redness and sensitivity

Usually a thinned barrier: too many actives, harsh cleansing, hot water, fragrance, wind, sun. The fix is subtraction: a gentle fragrance-free cleanser, a moisturizer with barrier lipids, mineral SPF, and actives paused and reintroduced one at a time. Persistent flushing with bumps across the cheeks and nose, or redness that comes with heat, alcohol or spicy food, can be rosacea, which is a doctor's diagnosis.

## Dryness and dehydration

See skin basics. Dry skin wants richer creams and gentle cleansing; dehydrated skin wants humectants under a moisturizer, fewer foaming cleansers and acids, and a humidifier in dry rooms. Both want SPF.

## Oily skin and large pores

Oil is genetic and hormonal; it can be balanced, not switched off. Gel cleansers, niacinamide, salicylic or mandelic acid, clay or sulfur masks, light gel moisturizers (skipping moisturizer makes skin oilier) and mattifying mineral SPF. Pores look smaller when clear and when the skin around them is firmer (retinoids, collagen treatments); they don't close.

## Fine lines and firmness

Lines come from sun, expression, dryness and collagen loss with age. Daily SPF prevents most of it; retinoids, peptides and vitamin C support collagen; hydration softens lines that are really dehydration; professional collagen-inducing treatments (microchanneling, peels, oxygen and tightening facials) build on that. Results are gradual, over months, and are kept with maintenance.

## Texture

Rough, bumpy or uneven skin from dead-cell buildup, congestion, old breakouts or dehydration. Regular exfoliation (acid or enzyme), retinoids, hydration, and professional exfoliation or collagen treatments for scarring. Small flesh-colored bumps that don't respond can be closed comedones, fungal folliculitis or keratosis pilaris, which look alike and are worth a professional look.

## Under-eyes

The thinnest skin on the face. Dark circles are shadows (hollowness), vessels showing through thin skin, or pigment, and each responds differently; puffiness is fluid, salt, sleep, allergies. Eye products hydrate and firm gently; caffeine and cold tools reduce puffiness short-term; peptides and gentle retinal build over months. Strong acids and retinoids stay away from the eye area.

## Back and body breakouts

The same acne, with sweat, friction and hair products added. Shower right after sweating, a salicylic or benzoyl peroxide body wash (left on a minute before rinsing), clean towels and sheets, no heavy conditioners running down the back, breathable fabrics. Body skin is tougher, so stronger exfoliants are tolerated, but still introduced slowly.

## Ingrown hairs

See the waxing file: exfoliation two or three days after waxing and onward, moisture, no tight friction, no digging.

## Blackheads and congestion

Blackheads are oxidized oil in open pores; sebaceous filaments (the grey dots on the nose that come back in weeks) are normal and can only be minimized. Salicylic acid, retinoids, clay, and professional extractions clear congestion; pore strips and squeezing stretch pores and leave marks.

## Hormonal breakouts

Deep, tender bumps along the jawline, chin and neck that flare before periods, with birth control changes, with PCOS or stress. Topicals help the surface (mandelic, benzoyl peroxide, spot treatments, consistent routine), but the driver is internal, so a doctor (dermatologist, OB-GYN or endocrinologist) is often part of clearing it. Supplements and medications for it are not the chat's to suggest.

## Melasma and sun spots

**Sun spots** are small, defined brown spots from cumulative sun. **Melasma** is larger, symmetrical, blotchy patches (cheeks, upper lip, forehead) driven by hormones (pregnancy, birth control) and sun, and it worsens with heat as well as UV. Both need relentless SPF, hats, and brighteners; melasma is stubborn, comes back with sun or heat, and is usually managed together with a doctor; aggressive peels and heat-based treatments can make it worse. Any new or changing dark patch should be seen by a professional.
